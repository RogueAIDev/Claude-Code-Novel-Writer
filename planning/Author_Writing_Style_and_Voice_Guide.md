# Author Writing Style and Voice Guide

P. D. Fox II

First edition

---

## **ABOUT THIS GUIDE**

This guide defines how the prose of *Chronicles of the Shadow Vanguard* sounds on the page: its narrative stance, its rhythm, its diction, and the way it handles romance, horror, humor, and interior life. It governs voice and nothing else. The *Series Bible* governs what happens, who the characters are, and how the world works, and this guide never overrules it. Part Eleven of the bible, the Drafting Guidelines, already fixes several craft rules (dialogue, romance, violence, atmosphere, viewpoint, terminology), and everything below is written to sit beneath those rules and expand them rather than compete with them.

The finished prequel, *Shadows of Loch Maree*, is the living proof of the voice. Where this guide and the prequel's established prose disagree on a matter of sound or habit, the disagreement is flagged for the author to settle, and neither side wins by default.

The guide applies at every stage of a manuscript's life: first draft, structural revision, line edit, and final polish. Part Twelve describes what to check at each stage.

The voice described here is a blend of four authors whose work has sold widely and earned unusual devotion across the genres this series lives in, which are Victorian gothic, dark historical fantasy, and queer historical romance. They are Anne Rice, Sarah Waters, V. E. Schwab, and KJ Charles. None of them is to be imitated. Each lends one or two specific strengths, and each also has habits this series deliberately declines. The goal is a voice that a reader of any of the four would find familiar in its pleasures and could not mistake for any one of them.

---

## **PART ONE: THE FOUR SOURCES**

### **Anne Rice: the body of the uncanny**

Rice gave the modern vampire a consciousness, and a sensorium to go with it. What this series takes from her is the conviction that the supernatural should be felt through the body before it is understood by the mind: the cold of a hand, the way a pulse arrives as sound, the terrible attentiveness of hunger. It also takes her seriousness about the moral life of monsters, the idea that a turned being can suffer its own nature and argue with it. And it takes the charged intimacy between men that runs under her best work, the sense that looking can be as dangerous as touching.

What the series declines is Rice's appetite for excess. Her long confessional monologues, her lingering on beauty for its own sake, and her romance of the predator all work against this series. The bible warns specifically against the brooding romantic vampire. Catherine and Calum are not seductive in their hunger. They are frightened by it, and the prose should be frightened with them.

### **Sarah Waters: the weight of real things**

Waters writes the Victorian world from the inside, through objects, labor, money, and class, and she writes queer desire under a legal and social threat that is never abstract. From her the series takes its material texture: the ledger, the chalk mark on a crate, the smell of wet wool drying at a fire, the precise wording a servant uses to a policeman. It takes her restraint, the way a single withheld sentence can carry more feeling than a page of declaration. And it takes her gift for the double reading, where a scene means one thing to the characters and another to a reader who has been paying attention.

What the series declines is her first-person confessional frame, since this series is told in third person, and any tendency toward a slow, inward pace in scenes that the seven-point structure needs to move.

### **V. E. Schwab: momentum and cost**

Schwab writes fantasy that moves. Her scenes cut cleanly, her chapters end on a turn, and her magic has rules that bite the people who use it. From her the series takes its forward pressure: short scenes when the story is running, a willingness to end a chapter on an image or a reversal rather than a summary, and the principle that every use of power leaves a mark. Amelia's blistered palms and Seraphina's darkened eye belong to this tradition.

What the series declines is a cadence that can read as contemporary, with quick ironic asides and a habit of isolating single lines as their own paragraphs for emphasis. The occasional one-line paragraph is a tool. A page of them is a mannerism.

### **KJ Charles: wit, law, and honest feeling**

Charles writes queer romance set in real British history, with the law, the class system, and the danger of exposure treated as live forces. She is also funny, and her lovers are funny with each other. From her the series takes humor as a form of intimacy, dialogue that crackles even when the stakes are mortal, and emotional honesty that never tips into modern therapeutic language. It also takes her rigor about how men of different classes actually spoke to one another, which matters for a duke's heir and his father's field operative.

What the series declines is the explicit bedroom scene and the compressed romantic timeline of the category romance. The bible fixes the romance as restrained and slow: no confession in Book One, the edge of speech in Book Four, Alexander speaking first in Book Six.

### **The blend in a single paragraph**

The house voice is close third person, anchored in the body and the material world, restrained in its declarations and generous in its observed detail. It moves like Schwab when the story runs, stops and looks like Waters when a room or a person needs to be seen, feels the uncanny through the skin the way Rice does, and lets people be dry, warm, and quick with each other the way Charles does. Beneath all of it runs the same current: things that cannot be said, and the cost of not saying them.

## **PART TWO: NARRATIVE STANCE**

### **Viewpoint and distance**

Alexander is the dominant viewpoint, as the bible fixes, with selected interludes from other characters at structurally important moments. Each scene holds one viewpoint from its first line to its last. The narrator sees what the viewpoint character sees, knows what that character knows, and uses words that character would plausibly think in. Alexander notices rank, manners, architecture, and patterns; he reads rooms the way he was trained to read tenants' dinners. Niall, when he has a scene, notices exits, weather, the placement of hands, and what a man's shoulders are about to do. Catherine after the Turning notices pulses, warmth, the haze of illness, and the slow erosion of her own name. Percival notices price, rank, and his father's opinion. Harcourt notices symptoms and then, late and unwillingly, people.

Distance should be close most of the time, sitting just behind the character's eyes, and should pull back only at the openings of chapters, where a short establishing passage can place the reader before the camera moves in. The narrator never knows more than the viewpoint character and never comments on the story from above. There are no sentences of the kind that begin "Had he known then" or "He would later come to understand."

**Open question for the author.** The tense of the prose is not fixed in the bible, and the prequel manuscript is not among the files in this project, so it could not be checked. This guide assumes past tense, which is the norm for the genre and for all four source authors. Confirm against *Shadows of Loch Maree* before the first chapter is drafted, and add the answer to the bible's Drafting Guidelines.

### **Free indirect thought**

Interior life is rendered mostly through free indirect style, where the narration takes on the character's own thinking without quotation marks or tags. Write "He could have said something. He had been not saying it for six years," rather than "He thought about how he could have said something." Italicized direct thought is reserved for rare, sharp moments and should appear no more than a handful of times in a novel.

### **Interludes**

Interludes follow the same rules as Alexander's chapters, held in one viewpoint and in close third, but each should carry a slightly different texture so the reader feels the change of mind before the name appears. Catherine's interludes run slower and colder, with longer sentences that lose their thread and find it again. Percival's are brisk, well dressed, and faintly anxious, with his father's voice pressing in at the edges. Thomas Barrow's are plain, careful, and exact, the prose of a man who counts linen. The difference lives in sentence shape and in what gets noticed, never in gimmicks of typography.

## **PART THREE: SENTENCE AND PARAGRAPH RHYTHM**

### **The governing principle**

Rhythm follows pressure. When nothing threatens, sentences can lengthen, gather clauses, and linger on a surface or a smell. When danger arrives, they shorten, subordinate clauses fall away, and verbs carry the weight. A reader should be able to feel the temperature of a scene with their eyes shut, from the sound of the prose alone.

Variety matters more than any single length. Three long sentences followed by a short one lands the short one. Six short sentences in a row stop sounding tense and start sounding clipped and mechanical. The same is true of paragraph length: a run of dense paragraphs broken by a two-line one gives the two-line one force, and a page of two-line paragraphs gives nothing any force at all.

### **Sentence openings**

Vary how sentences begin. Prose that opens sentence after sentence with the character's name, or with "He," or with a participial phrase ("Turning to the window, he..."), develops a drumbeat the reader hears even without noticing it. Participial openings in particular should be rare, and never twice in one paragraph. Start some sentences with the object, some with a place, some with time, some with a subordinate clause, and many with a plain subject and verb.

### **Endings of paragraphs**

Not every paragraph should end on a flourish. Machine-written prose tends to close each paragraph with a neat, quotable summary line, and the effect across a chapter is exhausting and false. Most paragraphs in this series should end on a concrete detail, an action, or simply the last fact that needed saying. Save the resonant closing line for the end of a scene or a chapter, where it has earned its place.

### **Periodic and loose sentences**

The Victorian setting invites the occasional periodic sentence, one that withholds its main clause until the end, and used sparingly it gives the prose a period flavor without pastiche. Most sentences should be loose, main clause first, because clarity comes first. A good rough proportion is one deliberately built periodic sentence for every page or two.

## **PART FOUR: DICTION AND PERIOD TEXTURE**

### **Period without pastiche**

The prose is written for a modern reader in a voice that respects the 1850s. The narration does not imitate Victorian novelistic style, with its direct addresses to the reader and its long moralizing digressions, but it never breaks the period with modern vocabulary, modern idiom, or modern concepts. The test for any word is whether a well-read person of 1851 would have understood it in the sense it is being used. When in doubt, check its first recorded use in that sense.

Certain words and phrases break the spell instantly and must never appear in narration or in dialogue. These include modern psychological vocabulary such as trauma in its emotional sense, processing a feeling, boundaries, triggers, closure, toxic, and self-care; modern business and social idiom such as reaching out, touching base, feedback, prioritize, impact as a verb, and okay; and modern science such as antibodies, viruses, blood types, adrenaline, and DNA. The bible's Period Vocabulary section sets the replacements for medical language: Harcourt speaks of the blood's property or principle, of corpuscles, of contagion and disease.

Characters should also think in period categories. Alexander does not think of himself as having a sexuality; he thinks of what he feels for Niall, and of what the law and the world would call it. Grief is carried in mourning dress, in rituals of letters and visits, and in a household's arrangements, not in a vocabulary of stages.

### **Concrete over abstract**

The single most reliable mark of living prose is the preference for the specific noun over the general one. Not a bird but a snipe. Not a building but a drovers' inn. Not fabric but wet wool, oilcloth, mourning crape. Not a device but an iron-cased thing the size of a pocket watch with coiled wire at one edge. Abstractions such as fear, tension, love, and dread should almost never be named in narration; the prose should instead produce them through what the viewpoint character notices and does.

### **Verbs**

Strong prose rests on verbs. Prefer the exact verb to a weak verb propped up by an adverb. "He crossed the room quickly" becomes "He took the room in four strides." Watch for the weak cluster of was, felt, seemed, began to, started to, and managed to, each of which usually signals a sentence that could be sharper. "She began to feel afraid" is almost always improved by showing the first physical sign of the fear.

### **Repetition**

A strong or unusual word should not reappear within roughly five hundred words of itself unless the repetition is deliberate and meant to be heard. Common structural words are exempt. This applies with special force to the words that describe faces and bodies, where machine prose tends to recycle a small stock of gestures. Characters in this series have specific physical tells established in the character bibles (Beatrice touches her brooch once, as someone checks a locked door; Niall's eyes go unnervingly still when he assesses a threat; Calum's hands open and close as if reaching for something). Use those, and invent new ones, rather than defaulting to jaws, breaths, and eyes.

### **Dialect**

The bible and character bibles fix the rules: Niall's Lowland burr is light on the page and thickens under strain, Calum's Old Town Scots is broader and more working-class, and neither is ever rendered in heavy phonetic spelling. Carry dialect through word choice, word order, and a small number of recurring forms (aye, lass, I'll not, ken where it fits Calum) rather than through apostrophes and dropped letters. One marker per line of dialogue is usually enough; the reader's ear will supply the rest.

## **PART FIVE: DESCRIPTION AND ATMOSPHERE**

### **Material detail**

The bible names the palette: coal smoke, gaslight, wet wool, rail noise, mourning crape, ledgers, the hiss of a galvanic cell. Atmosphere is built from things like these, chosen because the viewpoint character would notice them and because they carry weight in the scene. The bible also warns against decorative description, and the practical rule that follows is this: every descriptive detail must either characterize the observer, characterize the thing observed, advance the mood the scene needs, or plant something that will matter later. A detail that does none of these is cut.

### **One precise detail**

When a place or a person is introduced, choose one or two precise details over a catalogue. A room described by five adjectives is invisible. A room described by the fact that the fresh plaster by the balcony has not yet been painted to match is a room with a crime in it. The outline already works this way, and the prose should follow its lead.

### **All the senses, weighted toward the body**

Sight is the lazy sense. Gothic atmosphere lives more in cold, smell, sound, weight, and texture: the damp that gets into a coat's shoulders, the salt under stone, the sound of a lock turning two rooms away, the particular silence of snow. For vampire viewpoints the bible's rules supply a whole sensorium (pulses heard like rain on a roof, the haze of sickness, cold hands under gloves), and it should be written with Rice's physical conviction and none of her perfume.

### **Weather and light**

The outline gives most scenes a weather-and-light note. Treat these as instruments, not wallpaper. Weather should press on bodies and choices: a road that will close, snow that shows tracks, a dusk that drives a vampire indoors or releases her. Light should reveal and withhold. Lamps are turned up too bright in Harcourt's infirmary for a reason, and a refuge room underground has no daylight at all, which Catherine feels as a missing thing rather than notes as a fact.

### **Similes and metaphors**

Figures of speech must come from the viewpoint character's world. Alexander compares things to what he knows: horses, estates, ledgers, tenants, the rituals of rank. Niall's comparisons come from knives, weather, Edinburgh closes, and fieldcraft. Catherine's come from her atlas, from music she once played, from the life she is losing. A simile drawn from outside the character's experience, or from the modern world, breaks the viewpoint. Use figures sparingly, perhaps one or two a page at most, and make each one earn its place by being both unexpected and exact. A simile that has appeared in a thousand other books ("like a ghost," "cold as ice," "time seemed to stop") should be replaced with the plain statement or a fresh image.

## **PART SIX: DIALOGUE**

### **Subtext first**

People in this series rarely say exactly what they mean, and the most important things are almost never said at all. Dialogue should work on two levels: what is spoken and what is being negotiated beneath it. When Alexander asks Niall about the tree line instead of asking what he is afraid of, or when Niall says "That's a thing I can't teach you" instead of saying he is impressed, the reader hears both lines. The narration must not then explain what the reader has just heard. If a line needs explaining, rewrite the line.

### **Exposition through friction**

The bible's rule is that exposition comes out of disagreement, investigation, demonstration, or urgent decision. In practice this means no character ever tells another something they both already know, and no scene of explanation runs without resistance. Beatrice explains the Vanguard to Alexander while Sebastian argues he should not be told. Harcourt's science arrives through her argument with Alexander over the word "subject." Every expository scene needs someone who does not want to hear it, someone who does not want to say it, or a clock running out.

### **Forms of address**

Changes in how characters address one another are among the strongest signals the series has, and the bible calls this out directly. "Alex" belongs to Niall and Catherine alone. "Colie" and "Nillo" belong to one boyhood. Titles, surnames, and first names shift with rank, anger, grief, and closeness, and a single change of address can be the turning point of a scene. Track these deliberately across each book.

### **Tags and beats**

Use "said" and "asked" almost exclusively. Ornamental tags such as growled, hissed, breathed, and purred draw attention to themselves and usually signal that the line itself is not doing its work. Many lines need no tag at all when the speaker is clear. Action beats are useful for rhythm and for placing bodies in a room, but a beat attached to every line becomes stage business; a fair ceiling is one beat for every three or four lines in a running exchange.

### **Speeches**

Nobody in this series gives a speech that sounds written. Even Beatrice, who can fill a room without raising her voice, speaks in sentences short enough to be interrupted. Long uninterrupted dialogue, more than four or five sentences, should be rare and should be broken by the listener's reaction, a physical interruption, or a change in the speaker's own resolve partway through.

## **PART SEVEN: THE ROMANCE ON THE PAGE**

### **The governing rule**

The bible fixes the romance as restrained and specific, carried by practical care, private names, remembered habits, and respectful disagreement, and it states that the love should deepen the cost of secrecy and command, never relieve it. Everything in this part serves that rule.

### **Care as action**

Love between Alexander and Niall is shown almost entirely through what they do for each other and how they fail to mention it. Niall takes the draught from the door. Alexander checks the dressing on Niall's arm without a word after a day of silence. A knife lesson corrects a wrist, and a hand is taken away a moment too soon. These moments work because the prose does not underline them. The narration should report the act plainly and move on, trusting the reader to feel what the characters will not name. The outline's own phrasing is instructive: Niall does the thing "without comment," a habit so old that neither of them notices it any longer, and "the reader does."

### **The unsaid as the charged thing**

The tension of the series lives in near-misses of speech: the question Alexander has not thought to ask, the word "loved" that passes unexamined, the Gaelic entries Niall writes because they are things he will not say aloud. Build scenes that approach the edge and then turn away, and let the turning away be the event. Silence in this series is never empty. Every silence between these two men should feel full, and different from the one before.

### **What the romance prose must never do**

It must never state the feeling in narration in a way that closes the question ("He loved Niall, and he always had"), except at the points the bible designates for escalation. It must never use the stock vocabulary of romantic tension: electric touches, skipped heartbeats, breath caught in throats, butterflies, a jolt, a spark. It must never interrupt an action sequence to dwell on longing, which the bible also forbids. It must never let Alexander be at ease with closeness, since the character bible is explicit that he never learned how to be close to anyone. And it must never let the love function as comfort that makes a hard scene easier. The love is what makes the hard scenes harder.

### **The shadow of the law**

The 1850s legal reality, set out in the bible, should be present in the prose as a pressure rather than a lecture. It lives in where the men sit when others are watching, in what Alexander does not let his face do at a dinner, in the moment Amelia watches from a tent flap and chooses silence. No character explains the law to another. Everyone already knows it.

## **PART EIGHT: HORROR, VIOLENCE, HUMOR, AND INTERIORITY**

### **Horror**

Horror in this series comes from wrongness in the ordinary more than from spectacle: ink that darkens instead of running, a door left open exactly a hand's width, a father's face going slack at the start of a sentence he does not remember beginning. Write the uncanny with a flat, precise calm, reporting the impossible as plainly as the possible. The flatter the prose, the colder the effect. Reserve heightened language for the moment the character's control breaks.

### **Violence**

The bible requires violence to be frightening and consequential. On the page this means action written in short, clear, physical sentences with no confusion about who is where, followed by aftermath that is given room. Fights are over faster than readers expect. Their consequences last longer. Alexander's first kill must mark him, and the prose should stay with him in the snow afterward rather than cutting away.

### **Humor**

The series is morally serious without being relentlessly bleak, and much of what keeps it from bleakness is dry humor between people who trust each other. Catherine pressing handkerchiefs on two men "since neither of you will ever go anywhere to need it." Niall's flat "Your other pocket, Lord Percival." Humor here is understated, character-specific, and often a form of affection or defiance. It never winks at the reader, never uses modern irony, and never undercuts a moment of real grief.

### **Interiority**

Show thought through what a character notices, chooses, and refuses to think about. Alexander's interior life is disciplined and observant, and his avoidance is as telling as his attention (he passes the butler's pantry door without looking at it). When emotion becomes too large to avoid, render it first as a physical fact and only then, if at all, as a named feeling. A good rule for any emotionally significant moment: at least one concrete physical or behavioral detail before any abstract word.

## **PART NINE: SCENE AND CHAPTER ARCHITECTURE**

### **Openings**

Every chapter heading gives location and date, following the prequel. Beneath it, open in motion or in a precise sensory moment, not with weather for its own sake and never with a character waking up unless the waking itself matters (as it does in Catherine's interludes, where waking at dusk is the point). The first paragraph should place the reader in body, place, and time, and the first page should raise a question.

### **Scene shape**

Every scene has a turning point, as the outline records. Build toward it, land it cleanly, and leave soon after. Scenes should enter late and exit early. If a scene continues past its turn, the extra material usually belongs in the next scene or nowhere.

### **Endings**

End chapters on an image, a reversal, a line of dialogue, or a decision, never on a summary of what the chapter meant. The outline's endings are models: a threshold, a burning diary, a stone that answers to Sterling blood and goes untold. Schwab's lesson applies here with force. The last line of a chapter is a hook, not a moral.

### **Transitions**

Within a chapter, use a line break for a change of time, place, or viewpoint rather than a paragraph of travel or clock-watching. Readers accept jumps readily. They do not forgive a page of characters getting from one room to another.

## **PART TEN: HOW THE PROSE STAYS HUMAN**

This part catalogues the habits that mark prose as machine-written, so that they can be avoided in drafting and hunted down in revision. Individually, most of these are small. Together, they produce the smooth, symmetrical, faintly weightless texture that experienced readers now recognize within a paragraph.

### **Banned vocabulary**

Certain words and phrases are overused by language models to the point of self-parody and are never to appear in the manuscript. These include delve, tapestry, testament to, navigate in any figurative sense, realm in any figurative sense, symphony of, dance of, intricate, multifaceted, palpable, visceral, the weight of in figurative uses, a flicker of, a pang of, a wave of, unspoken as an adjective for a look or understanding, electric as a description of touch, and the phrase "something shifted." Sentences are never to begin with Ultimately, In essence, Indeed, Somehow, or Little did he know. The narrator does not use "It was as if" more than once in a book.

### **Stock gestures**

Machine prose relies on a tiny set of physical tells: jaws that tighten or clench, breaths released that the character did not know he was holding, eyes that widen or darken, hearts that pound or skip, lips that curve into smiles, fists that clench, throats that tighten, and chills that run down spines. Each of these may appear rarely if the moment genuinely calls for it, but the default is always to find the gesture specific to this character in this moment. If the gesture could be moved into any other novel without changing a word, replace it.

### **Structural tics**

Several sentence patterns recur so often in machine-written text that they have become signatures. The first is the contrastive reframe: "It wasn't just a gesture. It was a promise," or "This wasn't fear. It was something older." Use this construction at most once or twice a book, and never as a paragraph's closing line. The second is the reflexive triad, lists of exactly three adjectives or three clauses in a row ("cold, dark, and silent"). Vary list lengths, and let most descriptions have one or two elements. The third is the summarizing afterthought, where a line of dialogue or an action is followed by narration explaining what it meant ("He said it lightly, but they both knew the truth beneath it"). Cut these. The fourth is the rhetorical question in interior monologue, strings of "What did it mean? Could he trust him? Was it already too late?" Allow one question at a time, rarely, and let the character answer it or refuse to. The fifth is the atmospheric filler sentence set apart for effect: "Somewhere, a clock ticked." "Outside, the wind howled." Every such sentence must be specific enough to belong to this place alone, or it is cut.

### **Symmetry and smoothness**

Machine prose tends toward balance: paragraphs of similar length, sentences of similar weight, each beat given equal time, every emotion resolved before the scene ends. Human prose is lumpier. Some moments are rushed past and others held far longer than seems proportionate, because that is how attention actually works. Let a trivial detail get more space than a major event if the character would fixate on it. Let some scenes end with the feeling still unresolved. Let a paragraph run long because a mind is running.

### **Over-explanation**

The deepest tell of machine prose is distrust of the reader. It names the emotion after showing it, restates the subtext after writing it, and explains the significance of an image after offering it. The series trusts its reader absolutely. If a moment works, it needs no gloss. If it does not work, the gloss will not save it.

### **Punctuation habits**

The em-dash is the punctuation mark most associated with machine prose, and in this series it is rationed to a small number per chapter, used only for a genuine interruption in dialogue or a sharp break in thought. Commas, full stops, colons, semicolons, and parentheses do most of the work. Semicolons are period-appropriate and welcome in narration when two clauses genuinely belong together. Exclamation marks in narration are never used; in dialogue they are rare.

### **Names and pronouns**

Do not repeat a character's name in every sentence to avoid pronoun confusion; restructure the sentence instead. In dialogue, characters rarely use each other's names except for emphasis, reproach, or tenderness, which is precisely why the private names carry so much force when they do appear.

### **The read-aloud test**

Every finished scene should survive being read aloud. Prose that sounds natural in the mouth is almost always free of the patterns above. Where the tongue stumbles, where a sentence sounds like a greeting card or a press release, or where the reader's voice drops into a sing-song cadence, something needs rewriting.

## **PART ELEVEN: DEMONSTRATION PASSAGES**

The passages below are illustrations of the voice, written for this guide. They are not canon, and any detail in them that the bible and outline do not already fix (the kind of fuel in a fire, the number of heartbeats in a wall) is invented for demonstration only and must not be carried into the manuscript without a planning decision.

### **Practical care at the inn**

A version written in the machine register, to be avoided:

> The fire crackled warmly in the hearth, casting dancing shadows across the cozy room. Niall, ever the protector, moved to sit by the drafty door, his piercing blue eyes filled with quiet determination. Alexander felt a wave of warmth wash over him that had nothing to do with the fire. It wasn't just a gesture. It was a promise. Something unspoken passed between them, heavy with meaning.

A version in the house voice:

> Webb's fire smoked whenever the door opened, and the door opened every few minutes as the drovers came and went with snow on their shoulders. Niall took the bench nearest the latch. He did it the way he did most things that cost him something, without looking, already turning to shake the wet out of his scarf, so that Alexander found himself on the warm side with his back to the chimney before he had decided where to sit.

> He could have said something. He had been not saying it for six years.

> "Your boots," Niall said. "Get them off before they freeze to you."

The second version never names the feeling. It gives one concrete habit, rendered precisely, lets a single line of free indirect thought carry the weight, and ends on a line of dialogue that is practical, a little bossy, and entirely an act of love.

### **Catherine at dusk**

A version in the machine register:

> Catherine could hear heartbeats all around her, a symphony of life that both terrified and tantalized her. She was a monster now, she realized with horror. Tears welled in her eyes as she thought of her family. Would they ever accept what she had become?

A version in the house voice:

> She woke at dusk and was counting before she knew she had begun. The walls were full of them tonight. One heavy and unhurried, down at the lower door. One quick and uneven, which stumbled every few beats as if it had tripped on something. And one so faint and fast, somewhere under the floor, that she spent a long while deciding it must be a rat, and was ashamed afterward of how long.

> She had not breathed since the lamp was lit. She did it now, on purpose, the way she used to practise a scale she had never liked.

The second version gives the vampire sense a body, lets the horror sit in her shame rather than in a label, and reaches for a comparison drawn from her own lost life.

### **Exposition through friction**

> "The subject presented with—"

> "Her name is Catherine."

> Harcourt did not look up from the brush. "I am aware of her name, your lordship. I have treated a great many subjects who were somebody's sister. Precision will serve her better than sentiment." She turned the brush a quarter-turn under the lens. Then, after a moment, without any change in her voice: "Catherine." She wrote something down.

The facts of the scene arrive inside a disagreement, the disagreement reveals both characters, and the turn in Harcourt is carried by a single repeated word and an action, with no narration explaining what has changed.

## **PART TWELVE: THE VOICE ACROSS THE DRAFTS**

### **First draft**

The first draft is for story, scene shape, and viewpoint discipline. Hold to close third, one viewpoint per scene, and the outline's turning points. Aim for the right texture rather than perfect sentences, but do not defer the banned vocabulary and stock gestures to later; they are far easier to avoid than to remove. Mark any detail that the bible does not yet fix with a bracketed flag, so it can be raised with the author rather than hardening into accidental canon.

### **Structural revision**

The second pass checks each scene against its outline entry: does it turn where it should, does it plant what it must, does it end on a hook rather than a summary. This is also the pass for pacing, where scenes that run past their turn are trimmed, transitions are converted into line breaks, and interludes are checked for distinct textures.

### **Line edit**

The third pass is sentence work. Read for rhythm and vary it. Hunt the structural tics in Part Ten. Check repetition of strong words within five hundred words. Replace weak verbs, cut adverbs that prop them up, and convert named emotions into physical and behavioral detail. Audit dialogue tags and beats. Check every simile against the viewpoint character's world. Check every period term.

### **Final polish**

The last pass is the read-aloud pass, scene by scene. It is also the continuity pass for voice: forms of address, the private names and who uses them, dialect markers held at a consistent light touch, recurring physical tells, and terminology from the bible (Sterling, never Stirling; Duke and Duchess of Norwich; the Shadow Vanguard and the Office of Unusual Affairs). Em-dashes are counted. Any flagged detail still unresolved is raised before the draft is called finished.

---

## **FINAL PRINCIPLE**

The prose of this series should read as though it were written by a person who had lived a long time inside this world, who loved these people, and who trusted the reader to notice what they would not say. When a sentence is in doubt, choose the concrete thing over the abstract one, the silence over the explanation, and the character's own eye over any narrator's.
