# Preface

Shadows of Loch Maree takes place seven years before the opening pages of The Inheritance, the first novel of the Chronicles of the Shadow Vanguard. Readers meeting this world for the first time through the prequel will find Alexander Sterling a boy of sixteen rather than the man he becomes, and Niall Fraser new enough to his post that the weight of it has not yet settled into him. Little here will be spoken of again in quite the same terms once the main series begins, but nothing here is incidental. What passes between two young men in a training yard in 1845, and what one household chooses to conceal from its own children in the name of protecting them, will not stay buried. Some debts take seven years to come due. This is where they begin.

# **Chapter 1**

# A Letter From The North

## Sterling Castle, Norfolk \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

	Midnight brought a storm that laid siege to Sterling Castle.  
	Rain hammered the leaded windows in hard, slanting volleys, and the wind did not merely blow, it searched.  It found every hairline seam in walls that had stood for centuries, worried at old mortar, and dragged a low moan through the west wing like something wounded and searching for a way in.  Yet the castle held, its battlements and quarried stone unmoved by anything the storm could throw against it, sheltering the Duke of Norwich and his family within.  
	Inside the small records room adjoining the Duke’s private study, each draft that slipped under the soor set the candle flames leaning and guttering, as if the storm meant to snuff out the light before it reached anything worth seeing.  
	Niall Fraser bent lower over his ledger, as though proximity to the page might drown out the storm’s insistence that something was owed to it tonight.  Twenty-two days.  That was the whole of his life at Sterling Castle, though it had begun to feel considerably longer.  To the household, he was Mr. Fraser, the newest junior assistant to the Duke of Norwich’s private secretariat, trusted with antiquarian correspondence, collection inventories, and the sort of confidential travel arrangements that made senior servants lower their voices when he passed.  To the only three people who knew what that role actually concealed, he was something else entirely: a probationary operative of the Shadow Vanguard, an organization that existed in secret under the Office of Unusual Affairs, which answered only to the Crown.  This position had, so far, meant dawn drills in the cold, a growing catalogue of creatures he had not known existed a month before, hours training with a blade until his hand would not stop shaking, and an ever-lengthening list of doors he was not yet permitted to open.  
	He had glimpsed the Duke’s son only twice in three weeks.  Once crossing the upper gallery with a book tucked under one arm.  Once astride a bay gelding in the lower yard, too distant to hail even if propriety had allowed it.  Niall had yet to encounter the younger sister, Catherine.  She existed to him only as a name mentioned in passing and a portrait he had walked by a dozen times without yet knowing the person whose face was painted on the canvas.   They had never spoken, any of them, and Niall intended to keep it that way.  Whatever secret the household guarded, they were not his to carry into a schoolroom or nursery.  His work belonged to the Duke and Duchess of Norwich, Edward and Amelia Sterling.  The children were to know nothing of it at all.  
	Everything he had been given, he had been given on trust and waiting.  He had proven himself with a ledger, a blade, and a closed mouth.  
	No one had sent him into the field.  
	No one had so much as hinted that they intended to.  
	The ledger before him held nothing that still required his attention.  He had been set to verify the Gaelic place names on a shipment of artifacts newly arrived from a northern estate and to check the seals against an older catalogue.  He had finished the work the better part of an hour ago.  Since then he had copied the same line twice for no reason he could name, blotted the margin, and drawn one of his throwing knives to walk it across the knuckles of his left hand, under the desk where no one could see.  
	Forefinger.  Idle finger.  Back again.  The blade never touched the wood above.  
	Three sharp notes rang from the brass bell in the Duke’s study.  
	The knife stopped dead in his hand.  Niall was on his feet before the second note faded, the blade already vanishing into the sheath beneath his coat, a fresh notebook snatched up in the same motion that carried him across the records room at a pace just short of a run.  Cuffs still unbuttoned.  Mind already a room ahead of his feet.  
	The heavy oak door stood slightly ajar, warm light spilling through the gap and carrying with it the scent of tobacco and peat smoke, ordinary smells, comforting ones.  Niall knocked once.  
“Enter,” Duke Edward Sterling called.  
The Duke sat behind his broad oak desk, still dressed despite the hour, as though he had never intended to sleep at all.  Candlelight sharpened the planes of his face and silvered the dark hair at his temples.  In one scarred hand he held a letter, crumpled hard enough that the paper had begun to tear along one fold.  
A young Highlander stood before him.  It was the scent of fear that reached NIall first as he stepped inside, the stink of sweat, wet wool, and rain-soaked earth, utterly out of place among the tobacco and peat smoke, as though the storm itself had followed him through the door.  The boy, dripping onto a rug worth more than his family likely saw in a year, could not have been more than seventeen, mud climbing to his knees and his coat had gone nearly black with rain.  Exhaustion had hollowed his face until his eyes seemed too large for it, and beneath that exhaustion ran something worse.  He was trembling so violently that the wet brass buttons at his throat clicked together, a small, frantic sound, like something trying to escape.  
“Mr. Fraser,” Edward said.  “Take down his account.  Precisely.”  
“Your Grace,” Niall acknowledged.  
Niall moved to the small writing table beside the cold hearth, opened the notebook, and dipped his pen.  The messenger caught the soft Scottish burr in Niall’s response and seemed to draw an easier breath for it.  
“Your name?” Edward asked.  “Amd where have you ridden from?”  
“Ewan Macrae, sir.  From the eastern shore of Loch Maree”  
“Begin with the first death.”  
Ewan swallowed.  “Morag Campbell.  The shepherd’s lass.  She went down to the reeds near dusk to fetch her brother’s boat.  They found her after sunrise, drowned, caught between the stones in the shallows.”  He stared at the fireless grate as he continued.  “Her feet were… Ruined, Your Grace.  Skin torn away ‘round the ankles, right down to the white.  The village says Rhos-the water horse took her.”  
The old stories stirred in Niall’s memory even as his pen kept moving: black water, an inviting back, skin that turned to glue under a rider’s hands.  A Kelpie dragged its prey below the surface and held it there.  
None of the stories spoke of flayed ankles.  
“The second death,” Edward commanded.  
“Laird Macrae’s nephew, Iain.  Three days past.  He went looking for proof.  Swore he’s put a ball through the beast and hang its head above the hall.”  Ewan’s mouth tightened.  “They found him farther north, near a torn patch of reeds.  Discovered the same.  His wrists were cut to ribbons.”  
“But no bite wounds, on either of them?”  Niall asked.  
Ewan glanced at Edward first.  
The Duke gave a small nod.  
“No, sir.  Bruises.  Cuts.  Morag had a mark across her middle, dark as iron.  Iain had the same ‘round one arm.”  
“Hoof prints?  Or other unusual marks around the loch?  Grove marks in the mud, as from a cart?”  
Ewan’s eyes widened.  “Aye.  Two long grooves by the water, straight as ruled lines.  We thought the beast’s tail had made them.”  
Niall’s pen stopped.  
Edward sighed, a quiet, heavy sound, and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.  He felt t he familiar, crushing mantle of his oath settling over his shoulders.  *For Crown and Kin*.  The words were carved into the stone above the hearth, but they were more than a motto.  They were his blood-sworn duty to the Shadow Vanguard.  
Edward stood.  He walked over to a crystal decanter on a side table, the glass clinking sharply in the hushed room.  He poured three heavy fingers of amber liquid into a tumbler and held it out to the boy.  
“Drink,” Edward commanded gently.  “It will stop the shaking.  Or at least change the reason for it.”  
Ewan took the glass with trembling, dirty hands and swallowed it in a single desperate gulp.  He coughed violently as the fine scotch burned a trail of fire down his throat, bringing a sudden, flushed color to his pale face.  
Before the boy could catch his breath, the heavy oak door of the study creaked open.  
Edward looked up to see his wife, Duchess Amelia, stepping across the threshold.  She wore a heavy velvet dressing gown over her nightclothes, her dark hair tumbling loosely over her shoulders in elegant disarray.  To the untrained eye, she looked like a noblewoman roused from her sleep.  But her gaze was alert, calculating, and as sharp as a newly honed rapier.  
“I heard the horses screaming in the courtyard,” Amelia said, stepping gracefully into the center of the room.  
The temperature in the study seemed to drop a degree as she paused, pulling her velvet tighter around her neck against the sudden draft.  
“Good heavens, Edward,” Amelia exclaimed as she walked past him.  “I married a Duke, not a crypt keeper.  Must we sit in the freezing darkness to brood properly, or is it merely an aesthetic choice meant to terrify our guest?”  
“It builds character, my love,” Edward replied dryly, though the rigid line of his mouth softened by a fraction of an inch.  
“It builds pneumonia,” she retorted.  
Amelia didn’t wait for him to ring a servant.  She stepped toward the massive stone hearth, where only a few pitiful, graying embers clung to life under a mountain of ash.  
“Do stop looming over the poor boy, Edward,” she added without looking back.  “He looks as though he’s about to faint dead away, and I will remind you that is a silk rug.  Mud, sweat, and puddle-water are notoriously difficult to extract.”  
Ewan watched as the Duchess crouched before the hearth.  
For a moment, nothing happened.  
Amelia shifted one of the half burned logs with the iron poker, sending a scattering of dull orange sparks into the chimney.  Then she leaned closer, shielding the embers from the draft with one hand.  
Her lips moved ever so slightly.  
Ewan could not hear what she said.  
Perhaps she had merely cursed the stubborn fire under her breath.  Perhaps she had blown gently across the coals.  
But he could have sworn she made a small twisting motion with her fingers.  
The room seemed to grow strangely still.  
Then the hearth erupted.  
Ewan flinched as a rush of golden flame swept through the logs with a suddenness that made no sense at all.  One instant there had been little more than ash and dying embers.  The next, a blazing fire filled the enormous hearth, climbing eagerly through the wood as though it had been burning there for hours.  
A gust rushed up the chimney.  Sparks spiraled into the darkness above, and the sudden blaze threw wild, dancing shadows across the towering bookshelves.  
For one impossible heartbeat, Ewan thought the flames had followed the Duchess’s hand.  
His glass slipped from his fingers.  
It struck the stone hearth and shattered.  
Ewan stumbled backward, staring first at the fire and then at Amelia.  
She rose slowly and turned to him.  
There was nothing unusual in her expression, nothing frightened, nothing surprised.  She merely stood before the roaring hearth, perfectly composed, the firelight catching along the edges of her dark hair.  
“My apologies,” Amelia said smoothly.  She glanced toward the chimney before examining one of her manicured fingernails.  “A sudden draft, I suppose.”  
Ewan looked at the flames again.  A draft?  Of course, that had to be it.  
Still, he found himself taking another step away from the Duchess.  
“Sit, Mr. Macrae.  Before you fall.  Continue, Edward.  I find I am no longer tired.”  
Edward held out the letter.    
Amelia read while Niall summarized the account, and when he reached the injuries, she looked up at him.  
“Mr. Fraser, how does a Kelpit ordinarily take a human being?"  
It was not a lesson.  It was a test.  
Niall set down his pen.  “It lures the victim close, or lets itself be mounted. Once the human is on the Kelpie, its skin takes hold, becoming sticky, and then it makes for deep water.  The drowning is the point.  If it bites at all, the wounds would fall on the shoulder, torso, wherever a man tries to fight free.”  
“Would it strip the skin nearly from both ankles, or the wrists?”  
“No, Your Grace.”  
“Would it leave two straight groves through the mud near the shoreline?”  
“Not unless someone had taught it to pull a cart.”  
For the first time that night, the corner of Amelia’s mouth moved.  
Edward leaned back.  “Ewan, did anyone hear strange sounds, like machinery near the loch?”  
The messenger rubbed his hands before the fire, as though the answer required warming first.  
“Old Mr. Beaton heard grinding after midnight,” Ewan said, still staring at the fire and his hands.  “Thought it was chains on a boat, but no fisherman works that shore in the dark.  There were lanterns too, hooded ones.  And strangers were spotted two villages south, at the inn.  Englishmen, mostly.  Low carts, covered in tarpaulin.”  
“How many?” Amelia inquired.  
“Six, what I saw.  Might be more though.  One gentleman among them wore a fine coat, and he never took his gloves off.”  
Amelia returned the letter to Edward’s hand.  “The marks on the bodies are restraining injuries.  Morag and Iain found the equipment and were caught in it, or they were silenced for what they’s seen.  The wounds at the ankles and wrists came from fighting against iron.”  
“Silver as well,” Edward said quietly.  
The new warmth in the room vanished at once.  
“Silver, Your Grace?”  Ewan wondered.  
“You have done well to come here.  Better than well,” Edward said as he folded the letter, and with it, the conversation.  “Go down to the kitchen now.  Mrs. Thompson will find you food, dry clothes, and a bes near the ovens.  Speak of what you’ve heard in this room to no one.  If asked, say only that the Crown has taken an interest.”  
“Will you come, sir?”  
“We will.”  
Something in the boy’s face loosened at that.  He bowed, nearly lost his footing on the wet rug, and caught himself against the back of a chair.  
“Mr. Fraser,” he said as the door opened for him.  “They mean to drag the loch.  Villagers with iron hooks, gathering even now.  If there’s a beast down there, they’ll burn it before you ever reach it.”  
“Then you were right to ride as hard as you did,” Niall told him.  
Ewan nodded once and followed the footman who had been standing outside the door.  
The door closed.  
For several seconds, only the storm spoke.  
“Heavy iron restraints,” Amelia said at last, crossing to the desk.  “Silver worked into the lines.  Concealed carts.  Men willing to leave children dead because of their traps rather than be found out.”  
Edward’s scarred hand flattened over the folded letter as though he might hold the words still by force.  
“The Ashcroft Institute.”  
Niall’s fingers closed hard around the edge of his notebook.  
The name no longer belonged to a lesson.  Three weeks ago, in this same room, Edward had told him the truth of ihs father’s death.  Alasdair Fraser had not gone into the ground from fever during confidential Crown service, as his mother had been permitted to believe.  He had died from a rifle ball meant for Edward, taken while the two of them freed a creature from an Ashcroft transport.  The weapon had turned.  Alasdair had stepped into its path without being asked to.  
Edward had lived.  
His father had not.  
In the years since, Edward’s help had reached the Fraser household in careful, unremarked intervals.  School fees paid without ceremony.  Books sent north.  Quiet inquires after Niall’s progress.  Once, Niall had believed those were the acts of a friend. Now that he knew better, he had begun to fear they were the currency of guilt, paid out one year at a time.  
The ink at the end of his last line spread into a dark, shapeless blot.  
“Steady, Mr. Fraser,” Amelia said.  
He made himself let go of the tight grasp he had on the notebook.  “Yes, Your Grace.”  
Edward rose and crossed to a cabinet set into the wall.  The key that unlocked it did not belong to any ordinary ring.  It came from beneath his waistcoat, worn on a chain against his skin.  Inside lay a long leather case, which he carried to the desk and set down between them, unopened.  
“Ashcroft has hunted in Scotland before,” he said.  “They know the distance protects them.  They know frightened people reach for the oldest story at hand.  By the time a magistrate understands what he’s looking at, the creature is already in a wagon and the dead have been given to superstition.”  
“Do you believe the Kelpie killed anyone?”  Niall asked.  
“I believe we have no evidence that it did,” Amelia said.  “That distinction is the whole reason we investigate before we take action.”  
Edward opened the case.  Two solver-edged field knives lay inside, a compact pistol, waxed paper packets of ammunition, and a narrow bracer worked over with symbols Niall did not recognize.  
“Who are you taking?” he asked.  
Edward met his eyes without hesitation.  “My wife.  You.  Myself.”  
For a moment Niall was certain he had misheard.  “Your Grace.  I haven’t completed field certification.”  
“I am aware.”  
“I’ve never faced an armed capture party.”  
“Also true.”  
He heard the thinness in his own voice and hated it.  “Then why me?”  
The question hung there, larger than the room could easily hold.  
Edward did not pretend to misunderstand it.  “Your father’s name brought you to my notice long before you set foot in this castle.  His death placed a debt on me I will carry to my own grave.  But that debt is not why you are standing here now.”  
Niall waited.  
“At thirty paces you can place a blade in a circle no wider than a shilling.  You can follow a broken trail across wet peat.  You know the northern stories well enough to respect them, and well enough not to mistake them for proof.  You read Gaelic.  You know the ground.  And when you don’t know something, you have so far had the sense to ask.  Those are qualifications.”  
His gaze sharpened.  
“They are not a guarantee.  Alasdair earned his place in the Vanguard.  You will earn yours.”  
The words landed harder than any praise could have.  
“And if I can’t?”  Niall asked, looking at the case rather than at Edward.  
“Then we learn that on this very expedition.  Before someone else’s life depends on the answer.”  
Amelia folded her arms.  “There is another matter."  
Niall turned to her.  
“The institute killed your father.”  Her voice held neither gentleness nor cruelty, only weight.  “If you plan to ride north to avenge him, you’ll stay here instead.  Ander narrows the eye.  It turns a rescue into a reckoning, and it hands men like Silas Ashcroft the right to decide whose life is worth what.”  
Heat climbed his neck.  “I will obey orders."  
“That was not what I asked.”  
The windows rattled nuder a fresh gust, as if the storm wished to be heard on the matter as well.  
Niall thought of an iron net under black water.  A child kicking until wire opened her skin.  His father, choosing in one irreversible instant that Edward’s life mattered more than his own, and never living to learn whether it had been worth the trade.  
The hatred was there.  It had always been there, simple and hot and close to the surface.  
But somewhere at Loch Maree, something wounded was being run to ground by men and frightened villagers both, and if the Vanguard delayed even to indulge that hatred, revenge would become beside the point.  There would only be another body.  Another grieving family.  Another creature caged for a London cabinet.  
“I am going north to help whoever is trapped,” he said.  “Not to settle my father’s death.”  
Amelia studied him for a long moment.  “Remember that when the men in front of you wear the Institute’s crest.”  
“I will.”  
“You will try,” she corrected.  “That is the honest answer, Mr. Fraser.”  
“I will try, Your Grace.”  
Edward closed the case and slid it across the desk.  
Niall’s mouth had gone dry.  “And if I refuse, Your Grace?  What becomes of my place here?”  
Edward held his gaze a moment before answering.  
“You may refuse,” he said.  “No disgrace follows you if you do.  A first mission taken under coercion has a way of becoming a last one.”  
It was not the answer Niall had expected.  Niall understood that, even then, it was his choice.  But there it was: the open door back to warm ledgers, quiet drills, preparation without consequence.  
For one unsteady breath Niall nearly took it.  
He could feel the pulse in his wrists.  His mouth had somehow gon dryer, as dry as chalk now.  Every lesson of the last three weeks felt suddenly thin, measured against loaded riffles in a dark cave, and he understood, in that moment, that he had spent those three weeks trying to convince himself fear would not find him when the summons finally came.  
It had found him already.  It had found him before he’d even left the desk.  
“When do we depart?” he asked.  
“Within the hour,”  Edward said, his expression settling, though it did not soften.  “Wake the stable master, have the traveling carriage brought around.  We take the fastest road and rail together, and ride once the rails give out.  Pack for cold, for water, for bad ground.”  
“Standard field armament?”  
“The silver knives from the lower cabinet.  Your own throwing set.  Pistol and powder kept dry.  No blade drawn without my order, unless a life stands in immediate danger.”  
Niall lifted the leather case from the desk.  It was heavier than it looked.  Everything about tonight was.  
Amelia had already reached the door.  “And find something warmer than that coat, Mr. Fraser.  Scotland was not made kinder by the accident by the accident of your birth there.”  
“No, Your Grace.”  
She paused, one hand on the door frame.  “One more thing.”  
He waited.  
“You asked me the question that fear kept everyone else from asking tonight.  Remember that observation is a weapon too.  Very often it’s the first one we draw.”  
Then she was gone, her footsteps fading toward the private stairs.  
Edward remained behind the desk, the storms lightning crossing his face in flashes.  
“Sir?”  Niall inquired.  “The weapon that killed my father, was it theirs?”  
“Yes.”  
“Did you see who fired it?”  
Edward’s jaw tightened.  “I saw an Ashcroft rifle rise.  I sa Alasdair move.  I saw enough.  The man who pulled the trigger may be dead, or old, or drinking comfortably in London tonight.  It hardly matters.  The Institute remains.”  
Niall waited for more, yet none came.  
“We are not riding north to avenge Alasdair,” Edward said at last.  “We are riding because the living still have time to be saved.”  
“For Crown and Kin,” Niall said, tightening his grip n the case.  
Edward inclined his head.  “For Crown and Kin.”  
Niall left the study with a quicker-than-usual stride.  
Behind him the castle woke in degrees, bells sounding through the lower corridors, a stable boy shouting somewhere cross the courtyard, doors opening onto startled, half-dressed servants.  A single lit window along the passage held him half a step: the Duke’s son, bent close over some book he certainly hadn’t been given leave to keep past midnight, his brow nearly touching the glass.  Niall did not slow.  He took the narrow back stair toward the army two steps at a time, the leather case striking his leg with every step.  
He was nineteen years old.  He had belonged to this household for twenty-two days, to the Vanguard for scarcely longer, and he had never once heard a shot fired in anger.  
Before dawn broke over Norfolk, he would begin the long journey to Loch Maree.  
The Institute that had killed his father was hunting again.  
This time, it would find him waiting.

# **Chapter 2**

# The Bruised Iron Shores

## Loch Maree, Scottish Highlands \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

	The Scottish Highlands did not welcome then.  Their intrusion was merely tolerated.  
	Rail had carried the party north only as far as the unfinished web of iron dared to reach.  Beyond its last spur came a succession of hired carriages, post horses traded at wet coaching yards for beasts no fresher, and, at last, three hard-ridden mounts turned onto a drover’s track that the maps remembered better than the land did.  It ran where sheep and peat-cutters had worn it, not where any surveyor had planned it, narrowing by degrees until stone and heather pressed in from both sides and the mud kept better time than the road itself.  
	Once, where the track dropped through a scatter o low stone cottages, hardly enough of them to earn a name on any map, a length of black cloth hung nailed across one door.  A woman stood in the frame of a cottage beside it, a shawl drawn tight over shoulders that did not need it for the cold alone.  She did not call out, and no one in the party slowed.  For one instant her eyes found Niall’s and held, dark-ringed from nights without sleep, before she lifted a hand that would not stay steady and pointed north, toward water none of them could yet see.  
	Then the cottages fell behind.  
The gestures did not.  
Niall lost count of the hours somewhere north of the last railhead.  Sleep came only in fragments, and only when exhaustion stole it rather than offered it: his skull knocking against a carriage wall, his arms folded on a station bench, rain finding the gap at his collar while his body swayed with the horse’s stride.  Each time he surfaced, Edward was bent over a mao or murmuring low words to some stationmaster.  Amelia sat watching the passing country with a stillness that suggested she had not slept at all, that she had no intention of doing so until the loch had answered for whatever it had taken.  
Once, where the track ran level enough for two horses to walk abreast, Edward brought his gelding alongside Niall’s and matched its pace without a word.  Rain had stopped an hour before.  Only the drip of it, falling from bent branches, marked the distance between one hoof-fall and the next.  
“You have not slept,” Edward said at last.  
“No more than you have, Your Grace.”  
“That was an observation, not a rebuke.”  
Niall said nothing to that.  The quiet held until it became its own question, and something in the emptiness of the glenn loosened whatever had kept him careful through three weeks of ledgers and measured answers.  
“May I ask you something, Your Grace?”  
“You may ask what you like.  I reserve the right to answer badly, or not at all.”  
“Do you know why you truly brought me?  Not the reason you gave me in the study.”  
Edward’s eyes did not leave the track ahead.  “I gave you the true, honest reason.”  
“You gave me a ledger, a blade, and a closed mouth.”  The words came out flatter than he had meant them, worn thin by too many hours without rest.  “I do not know if that is the whole of it, or only what a man says when he has already decided to pay a debt and wants somewhere respectable to set the coin down.”  
Edward did not answer at once.  Whatever crossed his face in that pause, the failing light would not let Niall read it.  
“That is an honest question,” Edward said finally.  “It deserves better than what I have ready to give it tonight.”  
“That is not an answer.”  
“No,” Edward said.  “It is not.”  
He offered nothing further, and Niall understood, the way one understands a dor closing without a sound, that the question had not been refused so much as set aside, held for a night with more to spend on it than exhaustion and dark road.  
The land itself seemed to be waiting for the same answer.  Ground fog thickened without warning and thinned again for no reason Niall could name.  Wind combed through the empty glens as though searching for them.  Even the ordinary sounds of the road, hoofbeats harnesses, the creak of wet leather, arrived muddled, as if the country preferred to listen rather than be heard.  
Then the mist parted.  
Loch Maree stretched beneath a bruise evening sky, immense and dark, its surface flattened by the cold until it resembled a sheet of hammered iron.  Mountain slopes rose around it in blackening tiers, their peaks already lost to the coming night.  Strands of fog moved over the water like pale fingers, touching the shore and withdrawing again, as though testing whether it was safe to come ashore.  
No birds called from the pines.  No insects moved in the heather.  Even the horses had gone quiet.  It was that silence, deliberate and watchful, that told Niall more than any map had:they had arrived.  
Niall slid from the saddle and immediately discovered his knees had forgotten their purpose.  His arm shot out and caught the stirrup before he fell, turning the stumble into an awkward, feigned inspection of the leather, and hoped neither of his commanders had noticed.  
Edward noticed everything.  
“Walk before you try to stand still,” the Duke said as he dismounted himself.  “Your legs will remember themselves.”  
“Yes, Your Grace.”  
Niall led he gelding in a slow circle over the rough ground.  Feeling returned in painful needles from his feet to his hips.  He kept his right hand near the hilt of his field knife, more from habit than intention, though in his place, intention felt like the more honest of the two.  
Edward removed his traveling cloak and secured it behind the saddle.  Underneath, he wore a dark wool coat, reinforced at the shoulders and forearms, plain enough to pass for a gentleman’s riding garment until the fabric shifted and betrayed the hidden weight of leather and steal beneath.  
“Keep your hand near the weapon if it steadies you,” he said.  “Remember, thought: do not draw unless I give the order, or a life is in immediate danger.”  
Niall released the hilt at once.  “I remember.”  
“Then demonstrate it.”  
Amelia dismounted without assistance.  The journey had drained the color from her face but none of the precision from her graceful movement, and even travel-worn, she carried herself like a oman who had never once needed a hand offered.  Nothing in her appearance, the braided hair, the low hat, the riding coat cut close over dark leathers, suggested the woman who could kindle a dead hearth with a turn of her fingers.  Only her eyes gave her away.  They had fixed on the loch with an attention so complete that the rest of the world seemed to have simply stopped.  
Niall had watched her work small wonders during his instruction: a candle lit without flint, ink thawed in an unheated archive, a thread of green corruption drawn out of an old bronze reliquary.  Each act had been contained, almost domestic action, more refined skill than sorcery.  
Here, before miles of black water, she looked different.  
Not more magical.  
More dangerous.  
Niall busied himself tying the horses under the lee of a wind-twisted pine.  The animals pressed closer together, ears turned toward the loch, refusing to settle no matter how he stroked their necks or tried to soothe them.  
“They smell blood,” Edward said.  
Niall looked toward the reeds.  “Human or creature?”  
“Both, I expect.”  
The shoreline showed the violence even before they reached it.  Reeds has been crushed flat over a broad area.  Mud was churned into deep troughs, some irregular where feet had slipped and struggled, others unnaturally straight.  Dark stains marked the stones above the waterline.  
Edward crouched beside the first furrow and removed one glove.  He pressed two fingers into the mud, then traced the groove toward the loch.  
“What do you see, Mr. Fraser?”  
The question was not a courtesy.  Edward has asked it in training yards, archives, and once in a pantry after rearranging six ordinary objects into the evidence of an imaginary burglary.  Niall forced his exhaustion aside and studied the ground where Edward’s fingers were buried to the first knuckle.  
“Two kinds of disturbances,” he said.  “The shallow marks near the bank came from several people moving back and forth.  Heavy boots.  The deeper channels run straight into the water.  Something was dragged.”  
“Something?”  
Niall knelt at the second groove.  The mud had retained a repeating pattern: narrow ridges, then a deeper cut, narrow ridges again.  “Chain links.  Under tension.”  
Edward nodded once.  “Continue.”  
A tuft of brown wool clung to a broken reed.  Niall eased it free.  It was coarse, homespun, and stiff with dried blood.  A small horn button remained attached to one edge.  
He remembered Ewan’s description of the shepherd’s daughter.  
“A person was caught here,” Niall said.  “She tried to brace against the ban while the chain pulled her toward the loch.”  
Amelia moved closer.  “And the skin stripped from her ankles?”  
Niall looked at the parallel furrows, then at the blood on the reeds.  “Wire netting.  She became tangled and kicked against it as she was pulled under.”  
The words turned his stomach.  They also quieted the part of him that had spent the journey imagining a horse-shaped predator rushing from the water with a child’s hand between its teeth.  A folktale could be terrifying.  Machinery like this, and the men who built it, were worse.  
Machinery meant planning.  
Edward rose.  “There is no sign that a large animal came onto the bank.”  
“A Kelpie need not leave the water to drown someone,” Niall said, then immediately wondered whether he had contradicted his commander.  
Edward only looked at him.  “True.  What would it leave?”  
Niall searched his memory.  His father had collected HIghland accounts, separating tavern inventions from repeated details.  Niall had read those notebooks until the paper softened at the corners.  
“A sudden drop in the waterline if it displaced enough water.  Hoof damage in the shallows.  Weed or scale fragments.  Bite marks if it seized a struggling victim.”  He looked again.  “None of those are here.”  
“Not none,” Amelia said.  
She stood at the edge of the loch, watching a dark stain drift below the surface.  It moved against the faint pull of the current, then vanished under the reflected sky.  
Edward joined her.  “Can you read it?”  
“Perhaps.  The storm has disturbed the surface memory, and iron fouls water more thoroughly than blood.”  She removed her gloves one finger at a time.  “But something beneath us does not belong.  
A prickle moved across Niall’s skin.  
Amelia handed him the gloves.  “Hold these, please.”  
He accepted them automatically.  “What should I do?”  
“Remain behind the Duke.  Do not enter the water.  If I lose my balance, you will not touch me until Edward tells you it is safe to do so.”  
Niall glanced at Edward.  
“Obey her instructions exactly,” the Duke said.  
Amelia stepped into the shallows.  
The water covered the toes of her boots, then her ankles.  She stopped where the reeds thinned and raised both hands, palms lowered toward the loch.  
Niall recognized the beginning.  In the archive, when she had tested the reliquary, she had spread her fingers in the same deliberate pattern and drawn one slow breath through her nose.  The air had tightened.  Dust had lifted from the table in a narrow ring.  
Now, the fog stopped moving.  
Not gradually.  One instant it drifted across the loch.  The next it hung motionless above the surface.  
Amelis spoke a word Niall had heard before, though never loudly enough to distinguish its shape.  In the archive it had sounded like a secret whispered in a lover’s ear.  Here, it rolled over the water with the low resonance of distant thunder.  
The loch answered.  
Ripples spread outward from her boots, perfectly circular despite the stones and reeds.  A second ring followed, then a third.  They widened across the black surface until Niall could not longer see their edges.  
The temperature fell.  His breath whitened.  Frost silvered the grass around the loch.  
Amelia’s braids lifted from her back as though suspended in unseen water.  She turned one hand, and the dark spot beneath the surface returned, larger now, pulled upward from the depths.  Images flashed inside it, imperfect and colorless: a child’s leg thrashing among wires.  A hooded lantern swinging.  Gloved hands turning a crank.  Something enormous twisting below the water as silver sparks crawled across its scaled flesh.  
Niall took an involuntary step forward.  
Edward’s arm barred his path.  
“Stay.”  
The vision broke apart.  Amelia gasped but did not lower her hands.  
Her eyes had changed.  
The green irises were gone. Black filled them from edge to edge, reflecting neither sky nor water.  Niall had been told that deep workings altered the appearance of certain practitioners.  He had understood the statement as information.  Understanding it while those eyes looked upon the loch was another matter  
Something ancient seemed to be staring through the Duchess’s face.  
Niall’s fingers closed around her gloves.  
*I knew she was powerful.*  He thought.  *I had not realized she was also dangerous.*  
Amelia bent both arms as though gripping a weight below the surface.  
The water thirty yards from the shore began to churn.  
At first it resembled the bubbling of a spring.  Then a broad section of the loch heaved upward.  Black water rose in a mound, broke, and exploded into the air as a mass of iron tore free from the mud at the bottom of the loch.  
Chains screamed against the stone.  
Niall flinched as a weighted net, anchors, and a part of a toothed winding drum arced toward the shore.  The contraption struck the rocks with a crash that shook water from the reeds.  One anchor split a flat stone into two.  The net landed half in the shallows, its metal strands withing as tension released from them.  
Amelia staggered.  
Edward reached her before she fell, catching her under the arms and drawing her back from the water.  The instant his hands closed around her, the suspended fog collapsed.  Wind rushed over the shore.  Waves slapped hard against the stones.  
The black left Amelia’s eyes in a slow retreat.  She pressed one hand beneath her nose.  When she took it away, blood marked her fingers.  
“I am perfectly capable of standing,” she said, though most of her weight remained against Edward.  
“Then stand,” he replied.  
She did, after a moment.  The effort tightened her lips.  
Niall still held her gloves.  He realized he had retreated two paces without remembering the movement.  
Amelia noticed that as well.  
For one uncomfortable second, her human gaze met his.  
“A sensible reaction, Mr. Fraser,” she said.  Her voice was slightly thinner than before.  “Fear becomes insulting only when it is allowed to become stupidity.”  
Heat rose in his face.  “I did not know a water-working could reach so far.”  
“Neither did I,” Edward said.  
Amelia gave him a look sharp enough to cut leather.  “Your confidence is inspiring, my love.”  
“My confidence concerns you, not the limits you refuse to admit.”  
She took the gloves from Niall and pulled them on to hide the trembling in her fingers.  “The apparatus was anchored more deeply than expected.  Ashcroft appears determined to improve my health through exertion.”  
The mention of the Institute, and the Aschroft name, drew Niall’s attention back to the wreckage.  
Up close, the device was worse than the vision had shown: braided iron cable weighted at intervals with hooked block, and threaded through it, a finer mesh of silver wire, barbed so any struggle only drove the metal deeper.  Copper contacts studded the inner liner, feeding two tarred cables toward a sealed housing torn lose from the loch bed with the rest.  
Edward knelt beside it but did not touch the silver.  
“Galvanic.” he said.  
Niall crouched opposite him.  A brass plate on the housing had been stamped with a sequence of numbers and a small emblem: an open eye above a cage.  
The Ashcroft Institute.  
He had heard it described once before, the night Edward first told him the truth of his father’s death, though nothing in that account had prepared him to see it struck into iron and copper on a Highland shore.  
“A battery would have been kept on shore,” Niall said.  He followed the torn cable through the mud to a set of square impressions where a heavy box had rested.  “They sent the current through the net after it closed.”  
Amelia joined them, moving carefully enough that Niall pretended not to notice.  “Not enough to kill.  Enough to convulse the muscle and prevent a transformation.  The silver weakens.  The current punished resistance.”  
“Experimental?”  Niall asked.  
“Deliberately so,” she said.  “The Institute calls cruelty progress whenever it has found a new instrument for it.”  
Something pale was caught among the barbs.  
Niall reached toward it.  
Edward caught his wrist.  “Gloves.”  
Niall pulled on a pair plucked from his belt before freeing the object.  It was a strip of leather no longer than his hand, pierced twice by the silver wire.  A child’s shoe strap.  The buckle had been torn away.  
He placed it on the rock between them.  
No one spoke.  
The dead girl had not been attacked by a creature.  She had stepped into a hidden weapon made by educated men, dragged under the water while they watched, or while they decided that retrieving her would expose their operation.  
Niall’s anger arrived without heat.  It was cold enough to sharpen every edge around him.  
His hand moved toward the knife at his hip.  
“No,” Edward said.  
Niall stopped.  
The Duke had not raised his voice.  He did not need to.  
“I was only…”  
“You were remembering your father.”  
The words struck too accurately to deny.  
Niall looked at the Institute emblem on the battery housing.  “They killed her and left the village to blame the loch and a creature from ancient stories.”  
“Yes.”  
“And they did the same thing before.  They hide behind distance and superstition, and when someone dies, the dead become part of the concealment.”  
Edward’s expression did not change, but grief moved behind it like a shadow under ice.  “Yes.”  
“Then why should I not be angry?”  
“You should.”  Edward said, releasing his wrist.  “But anger is evidence of conscience, not a plan of action.  Use it to remember what must be stopped.  Do not use it to decide who must die.”  
Niall forced his hand away from the hilt.  
Amelia had moved to the norn net.  With the tip of a narrow instrument, she lifted one strand and exposed something lodged beneath it.  
A scale.  
It was larger than Niall’s palm and thin at the edge, shifting from midnight blue to deep green as the light moved across it.  A crack split its center.  Around the break, the surface had blackened where the silver had burned it.  Thick, dark blood clung to the underside.  
Niall forgot the cold.  
“Kelpe,” he said.  
Edward took the scale with both gloved hands.  “Adult.”  
“Could it have pulled the children into the trap?”  
Amelia shook her head.  “Look at the blood.  The creature strick the net from below after it was already fouled.”  
Niall studied the torn mesh.  Several iron inks had been bent outward, not toward the water but toward the shore.  
“It tried to break the trap,” he said.  
Edward looked at him.  “Why?”  
The answer lay in the direction of the damage.  The child’s shoe strap had been caught near the shallow edge.  The heaviest deformation surrounded it.  
“To free her,” Niall said.  
The possibility silenced him.  
All his life, stories had warned children away from black water by giving it teeth.  A Kelpie was the hunger under the surface, the beautiful horse that invited trust and carried the unwary to death.  Some of those stories were true.  Edward had made that clear.  Kelpies were predators, territorial and more then capable of killing humans.  
But this one had been injured trying to tear apart the trap that held a child.  
Amelia turned toward the northern shore.  “There is more blood in that direction.”  
Edward examined the mud behind the reeds.  Boot prints cut across one another, but a fresher line led away from the loch: heavy military soles, the narrow heel of a gentleman’s riding boot, and two deep parallel ruts.  
“Transport cart,” Niall said.  
“Empty when it came down,” Edward replied.  He pointed to the depth of the returning tracks. “Still empty when it left.”  
Niall followed the trail with his eyes.  It curved along the shore toward a rise of black rock where the land folded into shadow.  
“They failed to take the creature here,” he said.  “It escaped wounded.”  
“And they followed,” Amelia said.  
A low sound carried across the water.  
It might have been stone shifting beneath the waves.  It might have been a horse calling from very far away.  The note was deep, ragged, and ended din a vibration that Niall felt through his souls of his boots.  
The geldings under the pine threw up their heads.  
Edward rose at once.  “Caves?”  
Niall searched the shoreline.  The maps had shown a series of clefts farther north, places where water had cut into the rock and old collapses had sealed some passages from the open loch.  
“Beyond the point,” he said.  “There are water-cut hollows and at least one deep chamber.  If the creature s injured, it may have gone there for shelter.”  
“A dead end,” Edward said.  
“Unless it knows a submerged passage.”  
“Ashcroft will assume it does not.”  
Amelia drew her gloves tight at the wrists.  The tremor in her hands had lessened, though her face remained pale.  “They will surround the entrance, bring the battery forward, and finish what they began.”  
Niall looked once more at the child’s shoe strap.  
He imagined the creature under the net, silver burning through its scales while it twisted toward a drowning child.  He imagined armed men pursuing it along the shore, not because it had killed, but because suffering made it easier to cage.  
The fear in him had not disappeared.  It had only changed its shape.  
The loch was no longer the threat he understood least.  
Edward drew his broadsword just far enough to examine the edge, then returned it to the scabbard.  
“We proceed on foot,” he said.  “Quietly.  Mr. Fraser, take only the equipment you can carry without noise.  Watch the ground and upper rocks.  The Institute may have left a rear guard.”  
“Yes, Your Grace.”  
“And remember your purpose.”  
Niall looked from the Ashcroft emblem to the dark line of caves.  
“Rescue,” he said.  
Edward nodded.  
Amelia passed Niall on her way to the horses.  “Good.  Hatred is a poor compass, Mr. Fraser.  It points toward whatever wounded you, not toward whoever still needs help.”  
Niall absorbed the rebuke without answering.  She was right, and that irritated him nearly as much as the gentleness with which she’d chosen to say it.  
They stripped the saddles of field packs, secured the horses farther inland, and covered the metal fittings that might catch the falling light.  Edward took the lead.  Amelia followed, one hand briefly resting against the rock as she gathered herself.  Niall came last, tracking the churned mud, the blood, and the heavy boot prints north.  
At the edge of the reeds, he found another fragment of the blue-green scale.  This one was smaller.  Fresher.  The trail led toward the cliffs, and caves beyond.  
Edward looked back at him.  “The mission has changed, Mr. Fraser.”  
Niall glanced over the black water, where the fog had begun moving again.  
They had ridden north believing frightened villagers might need protection from a creature of myth and legend.  The evidence at their feet told an entirely different tale.  
Edward turned toward the dark mouth of the distant caves.  
“We are here to save one.”

# **Chapter 3**

# The Inmost Cave

## Loch Maree, Scottish Highlands \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

	The trail along the northern shore narrowed until Loch Maree pressed against them on one side and black rock rose up against them on the other, leaving barely a boot’s width of dry ground between water and stone.  
Edward was in front, testing each foothold before trusting it with his weight.  Amelia came behind him, her dark cloak brushing wet stone with a sound too soft to carry.  Niall took up the rear, watching the ground the way he’d been ordered to.  Learning to read it meant something else entirely out there than it ever had on a training lawn in Norfolk.  
The Ashcroft Institute had made no attempt to hide its pursuit.  Heavy boots crushed the heather into the black mud, and the wheels of the transport cart had cut two deep scars toward the water.  Blood marked the stones at intervals, some drops washed thin and pale by the mist, others still thick, still fresh, catching what little light reached down into the cleft.  
Kelpie blood.  
The father north they ventured, the more often it appeared.  
Niall kept breathing, slow and even, though the effort cost him more than it should have.  To his left lay the loch, flat and black, its surface swallowed by the fog before it reached any shore he could name.  To his right, the cliff leaned inward at intervals, forcing him to turn sideways and duck beneath shelves of dripping stong, water finding the gap at his collar every time.  
	His sword struck tock, one muted scrape, gone before the sound could gather into an echo.  
	Edward said nothing.  He waited only until the last thread of it had died into the cliffs, then went on.  
No rebuke came.  Somehow that was worse.  
He had left the bulk of his equipment with the horses.  What remained clung close and cold against him.  He carried a sword, a pair of balanced throwing knives, a narrow field dagger, coiled cord, a shuttered lantern, a slim leather case of tools.  All of it had been arranged for silence on the training ground, and none of it, he was discovering, seemed inclined to keep that promise on a cliff face.  Every buckle found some new way to announce him.  
Ahead, Amelia lifted one gloved hand, stopping them in their trachs.  She folded three fingers in and pointed toward a second set of boot prints, tracing where they broke from the main trail and climbed toward a shallow shelf above them.  
Edward crouched at the nearest impression.  The mud inside still held a faint sheen, not yet dulled by the mist.  
“Recent,” Niall breathed, keeping as silent as possible.  
Edward tipped his chin towards the shelf, and the three of them climbed without another word between them, whispered or otherwise.  
At the top, they found no sentry, only a heel-mark pressed deep into the damp earth, the crushed stub of a cigar, and a square of oilcloth that had gone dark where a rifle had rested against the sodden ground. Heather lay flattened behind a broken outcrop. A man posted here would have watched the southern approach while the rock hid him from anyone below.  
Niall touched two fingers to the stone beside the mark. "He's moved on."  
"Or heard us before we discovered this outcrop," Amelia murmured, and did not sound reassured by her own words.  
Niall searched the fog, squinting as he tried to bring into focus anything hidden there. Nothing moved.  
Edward lifted the cigar stub, turning it between thumb and forefinger, and brought it close to inspect. The paper was pale, costly, faintly scented even through the damp.  
"Ashcroft," he hissed, dropping the cigar and grinding it into the muddy ground beneath his heel.  
They moved with silent urgency as they descended the far side of the shelf. Beyond it, the land folded inward, and the loch's voice changed with it. The small waves no longer broke openly against the shore but struck at pockets hidden beneath the rock, producing slow, hollow reports like distant doors closing one after another underground.  
The blood trail veered off suddenly toward one of those recesses.  
The entrance was barely more than a split in the cliff face, half-swallowed by fallen stone and curtains of black weeds. Water breathed in and out across the threshold, and with each retreating wave the opening seemed to exhale: salt, rust, scorched metal, and beneath it all something sweetly rotten that Niall's mind refused, for a moment, to name.  
His skin drew tight, muscles coiling under his coat.  
The cave should not smell of the sea, not this far from it. Yet brine clung to the air regardless, and the current slipping through the fissure moved with a rhythm too deep and too patient to belong to the loch above.  
"A buried channel," Amelia said, her voice so low it was hardly a whisper. "The water may run through older passages beneath the hills."  
Edward crouched beside the wheel ruts, marking where they ended just outside the cave entrance. Boot prints continued alone beside drag-marks scored by something heavy and reluctant to be carried.  
"They unloaded here," Edward whispered.  
Niall measured the gap with his eye. "The cart would never fit."  
"No." Edward rose. "Which means they carried it all by hand, and judged the labor worth the trouble."  
A sound rushed out from somewhere in the depths. It was something faint, metallic, and simply wrong. It was not stone, nor water. A ratcheting click. Then another. The unhurried turn of some mechanism that had no business existing this far under a Highland hill.  
Edward drew his broadsword an inch and let it rest there. Amelia's hand found one of the slim knives inside her coat, though the set of her shoulders told Niall that steel was not the weapon she expected to need tonight.  
Niall cracked the lantern shutter to a hairline opening, just enough to keep from putting a foot wrong on the cold, wet, craggy stone of the cave floor.  
Just as quickly as he opened it, Edward's hand closed over his, smothering the thread of light before it could escape.  
"Not yet."  
They went in blind.  
The passage sloped down as soon as they stepped inside, water running across the floor in a thin, freezing sheet that hid every change in the stone beneath it. The ceiling dropped low enough to bow Edward's head, and in places the walls pressed close enough that, no mater how carefully Niall angled himself through, the hilt of his sword found them anyway. It seemed impossible that a Kelpie could fit through such a passage.   
He kept one hand trailing the rock and followed the pale suggestion of Amelia's coat ahead of him. Down here the dark undid any ordinary sense of distance. Edward might have been five feet away or fifty, and every breath came back off the walls sounding closer, echoing louder, and less like his own than he would have liked.  
The bone-chilling sound of ratcheting continued somewhere below, and between its turns, voices threaded up through the rock.  
"Again\!"  
Then something struck iron with a sickening clang\!  
The cry that followed did not belong to a horse. It did not belong to any human, either. It began low enough to tremble up through the soles of Niall's boots, climbed into a shriek of tearing metal, and broke apart at the top into something choked and small.  
The three of them went still.  
Niall couldn't see Amelia's face in the dark, but he felt the heat gather beside him all the same, a sudden closeness in the freezing passage, as though a hearth had been lit two feet away.  
Edward's hand shot out behind him, finding Amelia's wrist and holding fast, with a firm and steadying presence.  
"Not blind," he whispered, low enough that it was for her ears only.  
The heat retreated, just a fraction. Then they began moving again.  
The floor tilted more sharply. Niall set his boot on what looked like solid stone and found only loose shale under the water's skin.   
His foot went out from under him.   
He caught the wall before he fell, but the movement loosened a handful of pebbles, which went skittering off into the dark ahead of him.  
One click. Two. Three. Then nothing.  
Niall stood braced against the rock and did not breathe.  
Below, a man laughed.  
"Hear that\!" one mercenary called.  
"Rats," another replied.  
"Then the rats can take a turn at the damned winch."  
Coarse laughter answered, tired at the edges.  
Edward's hand closed once over Niall's shoulder, not to comfort but to correct, and Niall found his footing, and began moving on once more.  
The passage widened by degrees.   
Ahead, the dark took on a dull orange stain, and smoke came down the tunnel to meet them, carrying pitch, hot metal, and the unmistakable char of burnt flesh.  
The orange deepened around the next bend. Voices sharpened into words. The winch's ratchet was joined now by the low hum of galvanic coils and the wet snap of current arcing between fouled contacts. It was a sound Niall had heard once already, on the shore, where the apparatus had been dead metal.  
Down here, it was alive.  
Something pale showed low on the tunnel wall as they passed, half-buried under a crust of dripping mineral. Niall's eye caught it before his mind did: lines cut deliberately into the rock, old enough that the stone had wept lime over half of them, closing into a shape no Ashcroft drill or chisel had left behind. It matched nothing from his training. No ward, no boundary-mark, no cipher he had ever been made to memorize. Edward's gaze snagged on it a beat longer than the dark or the danger warranted. He said nothing. Amelia, a pace ahead, never turned her head. Niall filed it away with everything else that night that made no sense, and went on.  
Edward dropped into a crouch and worked toward a slope of fallen stone, where the rock created a narrow ledge above the larger chamber beyond. He climbed without losing so much as a grain of shale. Amelia followed, one hand flat against the wall. Niall waited until she'd cleared the last foothold before he climbed after her.  
He reached the ledge and looked down. For a long moment the cavern refused to resolve into anything he could understand.  
It was surprisingly large.  The cliff they'd found themselves on could never have held it on its own.   Then they saw the ceiling, it vaulted up into a shadow on columns of black stone slick with moisture. A broad pool filled most of the floor of the cavern, its surface broken by submerged rock and the dark geometry of chains. Along the far wall a recent collapse had sealed what might once have been a larger passage, leaving only narrow channels where black water still moved beneath the rubble, patient, unhurried, going somewhere Niall could not follow.  
Pitch torches burned in cracks around the pool. Their smoke gathered under the roof in a low, restless ceiling of its own.  
Niall counted ten men at the shore before he found an eleventh, half-hidden beside an upper pillar. Most wore leather coats or work aprons over dark clothing. Three carried percussion carbines, one fitted with a revolving cylinder under the hammer, the sort of costly experimental design the Institute favored precisely because the law had not yet learned a name for it. The rest held iron pikes, weighted nets, or the levers of two great winches bolted straight into the stone.  
Copper leads ran from the winches to battery housings shrouded under waxed canvas. Each time a man threw his weight against a lever, blue-white current cracked across the wet chains running into the pool.  
At the center of it all stood Lord Percival Ashcroft.  
His coat was cut far too well for a cave, his boots polished far too carefully for the blood pooling underfoot. A silver-headed walking stick rested in the crook of one arm while he consulted a pocket watch, cigar smoke curling from between two fingers of the other hand.  
He looked less like a hunter than a gentleman mildly inconvenienced by the weather.  
"You've had twelve minutes," he said. "If the animal is not secured within five more, I shall deduct the damaged equipment from every man's pay."  
One of the mercenaries hauled on a lever. "She'll tear the rig apart if we tighten any more, my lord."  
"Then use the current."  
"Battery's near spent\!"  
Percival glanced toward the pool, bored more than concerned. "That is why Mr. Ashcroft employs men with strength and no scruples. Turn the winch."  
The mercenary obeyed. The chains drew taut.  
Something enormous moved beneath the water.  
The Kelpie broke the surface into the torchlight.  
Even wounded, she dwarfed every man on the shore. Her shoulders rose above the head of the nearest mercenary, and the muscle along her neck shifted beneath scales the color of deep water under storm-clouds. Reeds and black weeds hung tangled through her mane. Her eyes shone pale silver, bright the way a struck match is bright an instant before it either catches or dies.  
Every Kelpie story Niall had grown up on had promised him a beautiful horse standing alone at the water's edge, offering its back to the unwary, its hide turning to glue the moment trust was mistaken for safety. Predators. Water spirits that had understood, better than any human ever could, the uses of a stranger's confidence.  
None of those stories had prepared him for this.  
Her flanks had been torn open by the restraints. Silver wire crossed her chest and forelegs, each strand driven into burned flesh. One chain circled the base of her neck. Another dragged her left hind leg toward an iron ring below the surface. Blood clouded the water around her in slow, dark ribbons that would not settle.  
She struck at a pike with her teeth. The man holding it stumbled back, swearing. She could have reached him, Niall saw at once. The chain allowed that much slack, but she did not follow. Instead she twisted her body between the men and the far corner of the pool, and did not move again.  
Just then, two smaller heads rose behind her.  
The foals were no larger than young Highland ponies, though nothing else about them belonged to any pony Niall had ever seen. Their scales ran pale blue-grey, soft-looking, not yet hardened into anything that could be called armor. Fine fins lay flattened along slim necks. Their eyes caught the torchlight like moonlight caught under ice.  
One pressed its shoulder to the other's. Both trembled.  
Niall forgot the rifles entirely.  
"Foals," he said, voice barely above a breath, disbelief threading through it.  
Beside him, Amelia's gaze fixed on the pair, and the nearest torch flared without anyone touching it.  
Edward laid two fingers against her sleeve. Wait. He said nothing aloud. He didn't need to.  
Below, Percival stepped nearer the water's edge. "The smaller one favors its foreleg. Note that. We'll reduce the valuation if the joint's been permanently damaged."  
A man crouched over a packing crate, pen already making notations in a ledger.  
"Mother, adult." Percival went on. "Two dependent young. Possible injury prior to acquisition. Temperament unusually aggressive."  
The mother struck the water with both front hooves. A wave broke over the nearest man and drowned one torch. The clerk scrambled backward, saving the ledger a half-second before he saved himself.  
Percival wiped the spray from his cheek with one gloved finger, expression unchanged. "Add intelligence sufficient to recognize items of importance," he said. "That may be of interest to the buyer."  
Niall glanced at Edward. The Duke's face had gone very still, the particular stillness Niall had learned to recognize as anger too deliberate to let show.  
One rifleman worked the lower bank, hunting a clear line past the mother's body. A second held the western winch. The third, a man half-hidden by the pillar, watched the chamber itself rather than the creature within it. A guard, then, someone paid to watch the door, not the prize.  
Niall measured the distance without meaning to. Twenty-four yards to the lower rifleman. Perhaps twenty-eight to the man at the pillar. The angle from the ledge was poor but not impossible. The damp air wouldn't trouble a well-thrown blade at either range. Torch smoke drifting across the sightline was the only true difficulty.  
Training-yard numbers. Clean, obedient numbers that meant nothing at all until someone raised a blade at something living.  
Edward's whisper barely reached them. "Three firearms. One high, one west, one at the water."  
Niall nodded.  
"The man above watches the entrance." Edward went on. "He'll turn the instant the first strike falls."  
Amelia studied the winches. "The copper lines join beneath the western drum. Break that junction and the current dies with it."  
"Can you do that from here?"  
"I can do considerably more than that from here."  
"That was not my question."  
Her mouth tightened. "Yes."  
Edward's eyes went back to the pool. "Not while the rifles are free."  
Below, the mercenaries reset their footing at the winches. The mother held herself between the weapons and her young, sides heaving, each breath dragging out wet and grinding. The silver mesh across her chest glowed faint where the current had scorched it.  
Higgins, a mercenary with a scar through one eyebrow, prodded her shoulder with his pikehead. She snapped at him. He flinched back with a laugh that didn't quite hold.  
"She's still got teeth in her\!"  
"Naturally, Higgins," Percival said. "Pain preserves instinct long after reason fails."  
"And if she kills one of the young, thrashing about?"  
Percival checked his watch again. "Then we sell the surviving specimen and dissect the other."  
The words settled over the ledge like something spilled and left to stink on the stone.  
Niall's fingers closed around the rock beneath him. He thought of a child's shoe strap caught in torn mesh. Of iron links bent toward the shore instead of the water. Of a scale cracked open while a mother reached for someone else's child. The Kelpie had tried to save the girl the Institute left to die.  
The creature below was dangerous. Her teeth could shatter bone. Her hooves could stove in a man's chest, and Edward had never once pretended otherwise. Kelpies killed. The old accounts did not lie about that. But danger was not the same coin as guilt, and every violent thing she'd done tonight had been aimed at keeping iron away from her young.  
Percival lifted his walking stick toward the foals. "Cast a second net behind her. Take the little ones first\!"  
Several mercenaries hesitated.  
"My lord," the scarred man said. "If we go near them, she'll pull against the collar till it cuts clean through."  
"Then she weakens the faster for it."  
"She might die of it."  
Percival turned his head, mild as a man asked whether it might rain. "Do you imagine the Institute lacks taxidermists?"  
The man looked away.  
Two others lifted a second net from the stones, finer than the one already fouling the mother, edged in flashing silver hooks, built for something smaller.  
The mother saw it. Her whole body went rigid.  
She lunged hard enough to tear the western winch half an inch from its bolts. The chain at her neck snapped taut. The silver bit deeper. Current burst white across the mesh and lit the water from within.  
The Kelpie screamed.  
The sound went through Niall like something physical. A blow landed somewhere behind the ribs. Her forelegs buckled. She forced herself upright anyway, the silver in her eyes flickering, scales ripping along her shoulder as though disturbed by a current only she could feel.  
Amelia drew a sharp breath. "The silver is breaking her hold on the shape."  
"Her hold?" Niall asked.  
"Watch."  
The mother tried to rear between the hunters and her young.  
Her body would not obey her.  
It began at the wound beneath the collar. Her great scaled neck shuddered and contracted, her mane spilling forward as weed and coarse black strands became simply hair, dark and tangled with reeds. Her forelegs folded at angles no leg should hold, narrowing as the bone drew inward on itself. Hooves split into hands that struck the water's surface, human in shape now, marked across each palm by blackened silver burns.  
The change came in broken waves rather than one clean motion, as though something inside her kept losing its grip and having to begin again. The strong line of the Kelpie's back collapsed. Scales withdrew into water-pale skin, leaving dark patches behind like torn armor over shoulder and rib. The chain, no longer held by a neck wide enough to bear it, slid free and caught instead across one bruised collarbone.  
Water and hair and the ragged last remnants of scale-hide covered her as she went down on her knees in the pool.  
Where the water-horse had stood a moment before, a wounded woman knelt now, waist-deep in black water.  
She looked neither young nor old. Pain had stripped such distinctions from her entirely. Her features were sharp, strange, beautiful only in the unkind way a Highland storm is beautiful: without gentleness, without invitation, without the smallest concern for whether anyone found it so. Her eyes stayed silver. Fine scales still traced her temples and the back of her hands, as though the change had run out of will before it could finish its work.  
Every injury had followed her into the new shape. Burns crisscrossed her chest and arms. One shoulder hung lower than the other should. Blood ran from under the silver wire and vanished into the black water without a sound.  
The foals pressed close against her back. She spread both arms before them. Not a threat. A wall.  
A mercenary lowered his pike unbidden. Even the clerk stopped writing.  
Niall heard his own breath leave him and did not try to call it back.  
The woman lifted her face to the men holding the net.  
When she spoke, water and pain had roughened every word, but there was no mistaking them for anything but human.  
"Not the little ones."  
No one moved.  
Then Percival smiled.  
It was not wonder, nor was it even surprise. It was calculation, wearing a human face for convenience.  
"Speech," he said softly. "Remarkable."  
He glanced toward the clerk. "Amend the entry. Adult female demonstrates linguistic comprehension and stable human mimicry under stress. That will raise her value considerably."  
The clerk only stared.  
"Write it down\!"  
The pen returned to the page, unwilling.  
One of the net-men shifted his weight. "Sir, she asked us…"  
"It made a sound you understood," Percival said. "Do not mistake resemblance for equality."  
The woman in the water gathered her young closer, hands shaking, the silver in her eyes burning brighter with every step the men took toward her.  
Niall's fear of her had not left him. He could still see the danger plainly, the tension coiled through her fingers, the shape of teeth too sharp for any human mouth, the impossible strength waiting just beneath that ruined, borrowed form.  
But the question in his mind had changed its shape entirely. He no longer wondered whether she was capable of violence. He wondered what kind of creature would not be here.  
Heat gathered at his shoulder. Amelia's glove had begun to smoke at the fingertips.  
Edward's hand closed around her forearm.  
"Not yet."  
Her eyes never left Percival. "He has ordered men to net children."  
"And three rifles are waiting for the moment you show yourself."  
"Then remove them."  
"I intend to."  
Edward drew closer, voice pared down to almost nothing under the machinery's grind.  
"Mr. Fraser."  
Niall tore his eyes from the water.  
"Your task is the Duchess's right flank. Do not follow me into the center. Do not chase a man who runs. Watch the rifles."  
He looked again at the chamber: the high guard by the pillar, the western rifleman at the winch, the third along the waterline.  
"The upper man will have the clearest angle the moment Her Grace moves," he whispered.  
"Yes."  
"Twenty-eight yards. Perhaps thirty, if he falls back."  
"Can you make it?"  
On the training lawn the answer would have come without a thought behind it. Niall had put knives into smaller marks at greater distances a hundred times over. He knew the weight of the blade at his hip, the balance point a finger's width below the guard, the exact number of turns it would make before it reached the wood. But the mark below was not wood. It was a man who would move, and fire, and bleed, and perhaps die, because Niall's hand had told a piece of steel where to go. All his preparation offered no certainty at all. It only made the outcome his to answer for.  
"Yes," Niall said.  
Edward held his gaze a second longer than the answer required, weighing whether it had come from confidence or from pride.  
Then he nodded. "The shoulder if you have it. The throat only if you don't."  
Niall drew one throwing knife slowly enough that the sheath made no sound at all. The blade's familiar weight settled into his palm like something remembering its own hand.  
Below, Percival gestured with the silver head of his cane. "Tighten the collar once more."  
The winch-men hastily took up their levers.  
"Cast on the young."  
The net-men spread apart, drawing the silver-hooked mesh taut between them.  
The mother tried to rise. Her human legs failed her, and she caught herself against the stone with one hand, still placing her body between the net and her foals, even now, even like this.  
Amelia lowered her knife and opened her empty hand towards the western drum. A faint ember kindled beneath her palm.  
Edward slid his broadsword free without a sound.  
The high rifleman turned his head toward the first whisper of drawn steel.  
Niall fixed his eyes on the man's right shoulder and raised the knife beside his cheek.  
Edward waited: one breath, two, until the net left the mercenaries' hands.  
Then he gave the order.  
"Now."

# **Chapter 4**

# The Ordeal

## Loch Maree, Scottish Highlands \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

Edward moved before the word had finished leaving his mouth.  
He dropped from the ledge to the lower shelf in one motion, broadsword held close against his body, and struck the stone hard enough to send loose shale hissing away across the cavern floor.  
The flat of his blade caught the nearer man across the wrist. Bone cracked, sharp as a branch under snow. The weighted net dropped from nerveless fingers and sank into the water short of the foals. Edward seized the second man by the front of his leather apron and drove him shoulder-first into the western winch, the impact ringing through iron loud enough to carry over the machinery's grind.  
At the same instant, Amelia opened her hand.  
The ember beneath her palm narrowed into a white thread of fire.  
It crossed the cavern faster than Niall's eye could follow and found the copper junction under the western drum. For one suspended heartbeat, nothing happened. Then every lead in the restraint apparatus flared blue, all at once, like frost catching flame.  
The galvanic batteries burst.  
Glass shattered beneath the waxed canvas, and a violent report rolled through the cave on its heels, sparks scattering into bitter chemical smoke. The current racing along the chains died in the same instant. Before the winch-men could recover, Amelia turned her wrist and the white thread widened into a broad, controlled sweep of flame.  
It did not touch the men.  
It cut across the wooden supports under both drums.  
Timbers blackened, split, and gave way. One winch tipped forward with a scream of torn bolts. The chain at the mother's collar snapped slack and whipped across the water in a wide hissing arc. The second drum tore free of its frame and crushed the narrow restraint net beneath its own falling weight.  
The transformed mother cried out.  
It was not the metallic shriek of the water-horse. It was a woman's voice, raw with pain and terror, and it carried above the breaking machinery the way a single struck note carries over a falling wall.  
The foals pressed against her as the chain slid free of her burned collarbone. She gathered them close with both arms and turned her body from the flying fragments, shielding them with what little of herself remained unburned.  
"Ambush\!" Percival shouted.  
His cigar fell from his mouth and vanished beneath a boot. He rapped the stone twice with the silver head of his walking stick as if summoning servants in a drawing room. "Rifles\! Kill the witch\!"  
The lower rifleman swung his weapon toward Amelia.  
Edward crossed the distance before the barrel rose level. His broadsword swept up under the rifle stock and forced the muzzle toward the ceiling as the shot went off between them, fire leaping from the barrel, the ball shattering stone overhead and raining chips down across Edward's shoulders.  
The mercenary hauled at the weapon. Edward twisted, trapping the rifle against his guard, and drove the pommel into the man's mouth hard enough that blood and broken teeth spilled into the water together. The rifle fell. Edward kicked it beyond reach and turned toward the next threat without waiting to see the man collapse.  
Niall stepped from the ledge.  
He had chosen the landing point before Edward gave the order, a patch of dark shale beyond the broken western drum, clear of the pool and within sight of Amelia's right side. It had looked manageable from above.  
The distance changed while he was falling.  
His left boot struck first and slid. His knee found stone instead of shale, and pain flashed white up his thigh. He threw out one hand, caught himself, and nearly drove his own knife into the floor for his trouble.  
Training corrected the error before thought did. He rolled with the fall, turned the blade away from his body, and came up behind the ruined winch.  
For half a second, he believed he had recovered.  
Then the cavern became too much.  
The first rifle shot returned from every wall at once, and after it came chaos in earnest. Amelia's fire cast the chamber in a violent pulse of white and orange. Battery smoke clawed at Niall's throat while men shouted over one another and a wounded mercenary screamed beside the pool, and beneath all of it, threading through it, the mother screamed too, one voice tangled so tightly with the other that Niall could no longer tell where pain ended and terror began.  
The smell reached him a moment later: burned timber, wet iron, blood, spent powder, and underneath it all the sharp mineral reek of the shattered batteries.  
A bullet cracked into the fallen winch inches from his face.  
Metal rang. A splinter of iron opened his cheek before he understood he'd been cut, and Niall flinched behind the drum as sparks scattered bright across his sleeve.  
Watch the rifles.  
Edward's order held steady in his memory even as everything else in the cavern refused to. Niall forced himself upright and searched through the smoke.  
The western rifleman had abandoned his position when the batteries burst and was working along the pool now, hunting an angle past Edward. The lower man lay on his side with both hands pressed to his ruined mouth. The upper guard was no longer beside the pillar.  
Niall could not see him.  
He turned further right.  
Nothing. Only smoke and torchlight.  
A mercenary charged Edward with an iron pike. Edward caught the shaft under his arm, pulled the man off balance, and cut once across the thigh. The mercenary fell. Another came from behind with a bayonet fixed to his rifle.  
"Your left\!" Niall shouted.  
Edward pivoted. The bayonet scraped across the reinforced shoulder of his coat instead of entering his back. He seized the barrel with his free hand and drove his sword beneath the attacker's ribs.  
The blade entered with a sound Niall felt rather than heard.  
The man stopped.  
For an instant, the attacker and the Duke stood chest to chest, both perfectly still. Then Edward withdrew the sword, and the mercenary folded to the stones.  
Dark blood spread beneath him.  
Niall had seen bodies before. His father's coffin had been closed, but death was not a stranger to Edinburgh streets, winter illness, or the accidents of dock and workshop. He had never watched a living man's life empty while the rest of him remained upright.  
His stomach contracted.  
He looked away and immediately hated himself for it.  
The broad mercenary with the scar through his eyebrow, who had prodded the mother with his pike, dragged himself behind a pillar. Percival saw him there and pointed toward the upper shelf with the silver head of his cane.  
"Higgins\! The spare rifle\!"  
Higgins looked up.  
A weapon lay where the guard had stood only moments before, half-concealed behind a ridge of stone, the guard himself already descended toward the center, drawn down by Edward's attack. Higgins abandoned his pike and began to climb.  
Niall saw him.  
He knew what the man intended.  
He also saw the western rifleman brace his weapon across the broken frame opposite Amelia.  
Two threats. One knife was already in his hand. A second in his belt.  
The western man fired.  
Amelia turned her palm toward the shot.  
The air between them buckled.  
The ball did not stop. It changed direction with a shrill, rearing sound and bit into the stone at her feet. Amelia staggered backward. The black in her eyes spread until no white remained, and the nearest torches bent their flames toward her as if caught in a wind no one else could feel.  
She raised both hands.  
Fire moved along the cavern floor in two narrow lines. One reached the western rifle and wrapped the barrel in white heat. The mercenary screamed and dropped it as the metal reddened beneath his hands. The other line ran to the remaining battery housing and severed the copper leads before anyone could restore the current.  
Amelia held the fire there, between the hunters and the pool.  
It was no wild inferno. It rose no higher than a man's waist, angled carefully away from the mother and her foals, denying the mercenaries the waterline and forcing them back toward Edward.  
The control it cost her showed in every line of her. Her shoulders shook. Her jaw locked. A thin line of blood traced from one nostril, and she did not move to wipe it away.  
Niall stared.  
He had watched her warm a room by touching the mantel. He had seen her coax light from an unlit wick and hold it cupped above her fingers like something newly kindled. Those small workings had seemed almost civilized, refined into something courteous for a young recruit's benefit.  
This was something else entirely.  
This was power held on a chain by will alone.  
One lapse would set clothing, powder, timber, and perhaps the whole cavern alight.  
The knowledge frightened him more than the fire did.  
A man rushed from the smoke to Niall's left.  
Niall turned too late. The mercenary caught him across the forearm with the haft of a pike. His throwing knife flew from his hand and vanished beneath the fallen net. Pain numbed his fingers to the elbow.  
The man reversed the weapon and drove the iron point toward Niall's chest.  
Niall stumbled aside. The pike tore through his coat and scraped the leather underneath without reaching flesh. He caught the shaft under his injured arm, but the mercenary was heavier and already pressing forward.  
There was no room for the clean movements Edward had drilled into him. No measured distance. No command to begin.  
Niall stamped on the man's instep.  
The mercenary grunted. Niall drove his forehead into the bridge of the man's nose.  
Pain burst through his own skull. The grip on the pike loosened.  
Niall wrenched the shaft sideways and shoved. The mercenary lost his footing on the wet shale and went down hip-first across the broken winch. Niall kicked the pike into the pool.  
He should have followed. He should have secured the man, recovered the knife, and found Amelia's flank again.  
Instead he stood breathing through his mouth, unable to make his hands obey.  
The mercenary rolled away, groaning. Niall could hear the man's breath. He could hear the crackling wall of fire. He could hear water slapping against stone where the mother tried to move her foals farther from the fight.  
Every sound arrived separately and all at once.  
His arm throbbed. Blood from the cut on his cheek reached the corner of his mouth. The taste of iron filled it.  
He looked toward Edward.  
The Duke moved through the chaos with an economy that made violence appear almost orderly. He used the flat of the blade when a man could be disarmed, the edge when a rifle or pike made hesitation impossible. He did not chase those who broke away. He kept himself between the remaining weapons and the water.  
Amelia stood with her own heat, black-eyed and terrible, holding a boundary no mercenary dared cross.  
Niall had no place beside either of them.  
That thought entered him with the certainty of a verdict.  
He had come because Edward owed a dead man, because a desk, a room, lessons, and a uniform had all followed from the moment Alasdair Fraser once stepped in front of an Ashcroft weapon, and Niall had mistaken the opportunity for proof, had let himself believe that accuracy on a training lawn might mean something in a cave.  
Now his knife lay under a net, his arm was half numb, and men were bleeding because he could not keep track of three rifles.  
A boy playing at war.  
The phrase formed in his own voice, cruel and absolute.  
He was nineteen years old. Old enough to know better. Old enough to understand that courage borrowed from another man's memory was no courage at all.  
His legs would not move.  
Percival backed toward the tunnel while shouting orders no one followed.  
"Close the water\! Recover the young\! Someone put down that damned woman\!"  
The mother looked up at the last command.  
She was still in human shape, kneeling in the pool with one foal beneath each arm. Her wet hair clung across her face. Scales flickered beneath the skin of her shoulders and vanished again. Each attempt to change back failed before it began.  
One of the foals cried out.  
The sound was small and high, almost like a child trying not to sob.  
The mother pressed her forehead to its neck.  
Niall's gaze lifted from them to Amelia.  
Then beyond Amelia.  
Higgins had reached the upper shelf.  
He crawled the last few feet on one knee, keeping below the line of the ledge, blood running from a cut above his eyebrow where stone or fire had opened it. He found the spare rifle, checked the pan, and settled the stock against his shoulder.  
Amelia did not see him.  
Her attention remained fixed on the wall of flame behind the pillar, perhaps thirty-one yards from where Niall stood, and smoke crossed the angle between them in slow black sheets.  
Niall's right hand found the second knife at his belt.  
His fingers closed around the hilt.  
They were shaking.  
Not slightly. Not in any way he could hide by tightening his grip. The point of the blade clicked once against the metal mouth of the sheath as he drew it closer.  
Higgins lowered his cheek to the stock.  
Niall tried to step into the throw.  
His left leg refused.  
The fear had not passed. It had grown roots through him. His heart struck so hard the target seemed to move with every beat. His injured arm hung useless at his side. Powder smoke blurred the edge of Higgins's shoulder into little more than a suggestion.  
There would be no stillness.  
No certainty.  
No moment in which he became the kind of man who knew what to do.  
Beneath the powder and the blood, one thread of scent found him whole, damp wool and gun oil, the way his father's coat had smelled on cold mornings teaching him to throw. Alasdair's hands had never shaken.  
Higgins's finger settled against the trigger.  
Niall threw.  
The movement began badly. His foot slipped half an inch. His shoulder turned sooner than it should have, and pain pulled hard across his bruised forearm as his body fought to find its balance again.  
But the knife had lived in his hand for years, and years do not forget so easily.  
The blade left his fingers clean.  
It crossed the cavern through firelight and smoke, turning once, twice, silver flashing at the edge of each revolution.  
Higgins saw it too late.  
The knife struck high in his right shoulder.  
The point drove through the coat, muscle, and the leather rifle strap. Higgins screamed. His arm convulsed, wrenching the barrel upward as his finger closed on the trigger regardless.  
That shot broke above Amelia's head.  
Stone burst from the ceiling. Amelia flinched, and her wall of fire collapsed into a scatter of dying sparks. Higgins dropped the rifle, but the strap, pinned now to his shoulder by the knife, dragged the weapon against him as he twisted away from the pain. He lost his footing at the edge of the shelf and went over it, his cry cut short by a heavy impact somewhere below that Niall felt more than heard.  
For a moment, nothing above the ledge moved at all.  
Niall remained in the posture of the throw, his empty hand still extended.  
He had missed the exact mark. The knife had entered too high and too far back. On wood, Edward would have corrected the release before allowing a second attempt. On a man, the imperfect strike had been enough. It had torn the weapon from its line and kept the ball from Amelia's spine.  
That was all that mattered.  
Amelia turned.  
For one terrible instant, Niall saw nothing human in her black eyes. Heat rolled from her in a wave. Then she found Higgins, the fallen rifle, and the silver hilt standing from his shoulder.  
Her gaze moved to Niall.  
She gave one sharp nod.  
The gesture lasted less than a heartbeat.  
It held him upright.  
With the fire gone, the remaining mercenaries might have rushed the pool.  
They did not.  
They had seen their riflemen fall. They had seen Edward cut down the man with the bayonet. They had seen Amelia turn bullets aside and burn iron white without so much as touching the captives she meant to protect. Whatever discipline coin had bought from them broke all at once.  
The clerk abandoned his ledger and ran for the tunnel.  
The net-man followed. Another dragged the wounded western rifleman by the back of his coat. Two more threw down their pikes and scrambled after them, slipping in water and blood.  
Percival stared at the retreat as though betrayal had occurred solely to offend him.  
"Stand your ground\!" he bellowed. "The Institute will see every one of you hanged for this\!"  
No one turned back.  
Percival did.  
He spun toward the tunnel, gathered the tails of his ruined coat, and ran directly into Edward.  
The Duke caught him by the throat.  
Percival's walking stick clattered away. Edward drove him against the cavern wall hard enough to knock the breath from him. The edge of the broadsword came to rest beneath his jaw.  
Everything inside Niall narrowed to that blade.  
He was an Ashcroft.  
Not the man who had fired the weapon that killed Alasdair, perhaps, but the same name, the same Institute, the same polished conviction that living beings could be measured, priced, and broken. Percival had ordered nets thrown over children. He had watched villagers drown in his apparatus and called their deaths an inconvenience.  
Niall bent and retrieved a fallen dagger from the shale.  
The motion was almost unconscious.  
Percival saw him. His face, already pale, changed as Niall approached through the smoke.  
Edward saw him too.  
"Stand fast, Mr. Fraser."  
Niall stopped.  
The command struck harder than the pike haft had.  
His grip tightened around the dagger. "Your Grace, he ordered them to fire on the Duchess. He killed those children at the loch. His Institute…"  
"I know what his Institute has done."  
Edward did not look away from Percival. His sword stayed steady enough to shave the five o'clock shadow from the skin below the man's chin.  
Percival tried to recover his arrogance. "This is lawful acquisition. My father has patrons in parliament, permits signed by men above your…"  
Edward pressed the edge closer, and a bead of blood welled at the cut.  
"You set illegal restraints in protected water," Edward said. His voice had gone quiet, and the quiet carried farther than Percival's shouting ever had. "You concealed mechanisms that killed two Highland children. You attempted to remove a sentient mother and her young for sale. There is no paper in London that makes any of that lawful."  
"They are animals."  
Behind Edward, the woman in the water made a low sound.  
Percival's eyes flickered toward her and back. "Specimens," he corrected. "Resources. You cannot claim jurisdiction over every strange thing that crawls out of a bog."  
"The Crown can." Edward's expression did not change. "And through the Shadow Vanguard, it has."  
Percival swallowed against the blade. "Then arrest me. Parade me through a public court and explain precisely what I was collecting. Explain her. Explain your wife. See which of us survives the scandal."  
The challenge landed because it held a grain of truth. The hidden world could not be defended in any ordinary courtroom without being laid open to every government, newspaper, collector, and profiteer in Europe, and Percival knew it. His confidence crept back by degrees.  
Niall hated him for that confidence more than for the threat itself.  
"Let me take him," Niall said.  
He heard the anger in his own voice, and beneath it, how much it sounded like pleading.  
Edward looked at him then, not at the dagger, at Niall.  
"No."  
One word.  
Niall's face burned. "He will do it again."  
"Perhaps."  
"Then why spare him?"  
Edward turned back to Percival. "Because the immediate threat is ended," he said. "Because we came into this cave to save lives, not to answer murder with appetite. And because I will not teach either of you that power becomes righteous merely because the man holding it is angry."  
The words silenced more than Niall.  
Percival's returning smile vanished. Edward lowered the sword by an inch, though his hand remained locked around the man's throat.  
"You will take every surviving employee you can carry and leave this place," he said. "You will abandon the nets, rifles, ledgers, batteries, cages, and transport cart. The Vanguard will keep them as evidence."  
"Evidence for what court?" Percival asked, hoarse.  
"Not every judgement is public," Edward said. He leaned in close. "Tell your father that Loch Maree is closed to the Ashcroft Institute. Tell him the next apparatus found in protected territory will be destroyed along with everyone who chooses to defend it. And tell him Alasdair Fraser's death did not purchase silence from me."  
Niall went still.  
Percival's gaze snapped toward him.  
For the first time he looked at Niall as something other than another armed man in Edward's company. His eyes moved over the ginger hair, the empty throwing sheath, the dagger still in Niall's hand.  
Recognition did not quite arrive. Suspicion did.  
Something moved behind Percival's eyes, a man reaching for a name just out of memory's grasp. His mouth shaped the first sound of it and stopped, unspoken, before it could become anything at all.  
Edward saw it and shifted the sword back beneath Percival's jaw. "Your attention is here."  
Percival looked at him.  
"Do we understand one another?"  
The answer took several breaths.  
"Yes."  
Edward released him.  
Percival stooped for his walking stick.  
"Leave it." Amelia's voice cut across the cavern. Her eyes had returned to their ordinary color, though the skin around them still looked bruised from within. Blood marked her upper lip. She stood beside the ruined batteries with one hand braced against the stone.  
Percival looked at the silver-headed cane, then at her.  
He left it where it lay.  
The surviving Ashcroft men gathered their wounded with the haste of people who expected the order to be withdrawn at any moment. Two lifted Higgins between them, the knife still standing from his shoulder, pinning the rifle strap to his coat. He was conscious enough to curse when they moved him.  
Niall watched the silver hilt disappear into the tunnel.  
No triumph followed it.  
His hand began to shake again. The tremor moved from his fingers into his wrist and up along his arm until he had to release the borrowed dagger before he dropped it. It struck the shale with a small, ordinary sound, almost lost beneath the retreating boots.  
Percival was the last to leave. At the mouth of the passage he looked back once. Not at Edward, not at Amelia, but at the mother and her foals.  
Even beaten, he looked as though he were calculating their value.  
Then the darkness took him as the footsteps receded.  
One by one, their echoes failed to return.

# **Chapter 5**

# An Act Of Mercy

## Loch Maree, Scottish Highlands \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

The cavern did not become peaceful when the Ashcroft men fled. It merely became quiet enough for every wound to be heard.  
Water lapped against the shale in slow, irregular strokes. The ruined winch gave an occasional metallic tick as heated iron contracted. Somewhere beyond the bend of the passage, the retreating mercenaries dragged their injured through the dark, their boots and curses fading until the cave refused to return the sound.  
Nearby, the mother breathed like a person drowning on dry land. She remained waist-deep at the far edge of the tidal pool, one arm braced against the rock, the other spread before the two foals. Her human shape would not hold. Dark scales flowered across her shoulder and vanished under her water-pale skin. Her fingers lengthened, webbed, and became fingers again. The reed-black mass of her hair clung to her face and chest, preserving what dignity the Ashcroft nets had not torn from her. Every failed change opened the burns along her ribs and thighs anew.  
The foals pressed behind her, their narrow heads barely above the water. One made a soft questioning chirr. The mother answered without looking back, a low sound from somewhere between a woman's throat and the grinding call of the water-horse.  
Niall stood where his last throw had left him, empty sheath at his belt and blood drying along his cheek. His body had not yet accepted that the battle was over. Every nerve remained drawn tight, waiting for another rifle to rise from the smoke.  
The mother looked from Edward's sword to Amelia's scorched gloves, then to Niall.  
Her lips drew back. The teeth behind them were too sharp for any human mouth.  
Niall's hand moved toward the knife he no longer possessed.  
"Stand down, Mr. Fraser," Edward said.  
The command was quiet, but Niall obeyed before he had fully heard it. His hand fell to his side.  
Near the broken restraint drum, the mercenary Edward had struck down earlier gave a wet groan and shifted against the stone. Niall turned toward him.  
"Alive," Amelia said. She had one hand against the cavern wall. Her face had gone nearly as pale as the mother Kelpie's, and the dark hair at her temples was plastered with sweat. "And unlikely to trouble us for several minutes. Move the rifle beyond his reach. Nothing more."  
Niall crossed the shale, fighting the tremor in his legs, and kicked the fallen man's weapon from under his outstretched hand before pulling it farther across the rock by its leather sling. He checked the man's breathing anyway, because Edward had taught him to check, even when the wounded man had been trying to kill him moments before.  
The mercenary's breath rasped, but it came steadily enough.  
"He'll live," Niall said.  
"Then leave him the opportunity," Edward replied.  
The Duke sheathed his broadsword.  
The click of the steel entering leather made the mother recoil. Her shoulder struck the wall. Both foals screamed, and she bared those impossible teeth again.  
Edward stopped at once.  
He did not speak to her as one might soothe a frightened horse. He did not claim authority, offer promises she had no reason to believe, or advance under the assumption that rescue had purchased trust.  
He unfastened his sword belt.  
The heavy scabbard struck the shale with a flat, final sound.  
Niall stared.  
Edward removed the dagger at the back of his waist and set it beside the sword. A second blade came from inside his boot. He unclasped the silver-woven bracers from his forearms, stripped off his gloves, and laid those down as well. When he had finished, he opened his coat and turned slowly so that the mother could see there was no other weapon hidden against him.  
"Your Grace," Niall said, unable to keep the warning from his voice.  
Edward glanced at him. "What do you see?"  
Niall looked across the pool.  
He saw a woman with blood at the corners of her mouth, scales breaking through her skin, a clawed hand held between them and her young. He saw the violent shudder that passed through her each time the last of the silver fought the change trying to take her.  
He saw an apex predator capable of tearing a man apart.  
He saw a mother who had begged armed strangers not to take her children.  
"She is injured," Niall said.  
"And?"  
"Terrified."  
"Yes."  
Edward faced the pool again. "A cage teaches the captive that every approaching hand belongs to the jailer. We will not ask her to know the difference until we show it."  
He stepped into the water.  
The mother lunged.  
Her movement was faster than any injured body should have managed. One instant she was against the wall. The next she had crossed half the space between them, water exploding around her hips. Her right arm changed as she struck. Fingers fused, darkened, and broadened into the beginning of a scaled foreleg topped by a black hoof.  
Edward did not retreat.  
The half-formed limb stopped inches from his chest.  
The mother trembled with the effort of holding it there.  
Niall's breath lodged in his throat. Beside him, Amelia did not move, though her fingers curled once against the stone.  
Edward lifted his empty hands, palms toward the mother.  
"The iron is broken," he said. His voice was low and measured. He spoke first in English, then repeated the words in an older cadence Niall did not recognize. It was not quite Gaelic and not quite any language from the field manuals. The phrases carried the rhythm of the tide against rock: statement, pause, answer withheld.  
"The nets are gone. The hunters have left. We will not bind you. We will not take them."  
At the final word, the mother glanced back at the foals.  
Edward lowered himself in the water until he was kneeling. The pool rose to his chest. He bowed his head, leaving the back of his neck exposed.  
The cavern held its breath with him.  
The mother came closer.  
She moved one step at a time, her human legs unstable beneath her. When she reached him, she raised one clawed hand and pressed the tips against the old scars along his temple and cheek. Edward did not flinch. Her nails moved down to the pulse in his throat.  
A single flex would have opened it.  
Niall felt his own heartbeat in his teeth.  
The mother leaned close enough that her breath stirred Edward's graying hair. Her silver eyes searched his face. Whatever she found there did not persuade her to trust him. It persuaded her, perhaps, to delay killing him.  
For the moment, it was enough.  
Edward raised his head slightly. "Amelia."  
The Duchess pushed away from the wall, her first step nearly failing her.  
Niall moved without thinking and offered his arm. Amelia's gaze flicked to him. For an instant he expected the cutting refusal she might have given in the study at Sterling Castle.  
Instead, she placed two fingers against his sleeve and used him for balance.  
Her touch was cold.  
That frightened him more than the fire had.  
"Do not mistake assistance for the permission to fuss, Mr. Fraser," she murmured with a sly smile.  
"I would not dare, Your Grace."  
"A promising survival instinct."  
They reached the water's edge. Amelia released him and lowered herself carefully to one knee. The movement cost her. Her shoulders tightened, and for a moment she closed her eyes as though the cavern had tilted beneath her.  
Edward watched her across the water. "Can you do it?"  
"Yes."  
"That was not the question."  
Amelia opened her eyes. Their ordinary dark color had returned, but a ring of black remained around each iris. "If I leave the silver in her, the question becomes irrelevant."  
The mother turned toward her.  
A hiss rose in the creature's throat. The foals crowded tighter against the wall.  
Amelia removed her gloves. The leather had burned through at the fingertips. Under it, the skin of her palms was reddened and blistered from channeling the fire. Niall had seen those hands turn Ashcroft machinery to glowing ruin, watched flames bend around captives to strike only chains, rifle mechanisms, and men who would not lower their weapons.  
Now the same hands shook.  
Amelia placed them in the dark water.  
"I must touch what is poisoning you," she said to the mother. "I will cause pain before I take it away."  
The mother could not have understood every word. Yet her gaze moved from Amelia's injured palms to Edward's unguarded throat and then to the foals behind her.  
She did not consent in any human fashion.  
She stopped hissing.  
Amelia drew a slow breath.  
The water around her hands changed.  
There was no eruption this time. No geyser, no violent command that forced the loch to surrender its secrets. A faint blue light gathered beneath the surface, no brighter, at first, than moonlight under the ice. It spread in thin veins toward the mother, moving around Edward without touching him.  
When the light reached the Kelpie's wounds, she screamed.  
Her body convulsed. The unstable human shape broke apart across her shoulder. Scales surged down her back, one leg kicked out as a horse's hind limb and shattered water against the wall. Edward rose but did not seize her. He placed both hands against the stone beside her, giving her something solid to brace against without turning the contact into restraint.  
The foals shrilled.  
"Hold," Amelia whispered.  
Niall did not know whether she spoke to the mother, the working, or herself.  
Black threads rose from the wounds.  
At first he thought they were blood. Then torchlight caught the metallic glimmer inside them. Silver dust, filings, and fragments finer than wire drew free of burned flesh and gathered in the water like a storm of needles. Each particle resisted. The current pulled them in one direction while the mother's body seemed to drag them back.  
Amelia's blistered hands tightened below the water.  
The blue light brightened.  
Niall remembered the fire pouring from those same palms, remembered the instant after Higgins fired when Amelia turned and looked at him with eyes emptied of anything he recognized as human. In that moment he had understood that she could kill him before he managed to reach for a blade.  
He had thought danger was the truest measure of her power.  
He had been wrong.  
This required more.  
Destruction had allowed force. Healing demanded precision. Amelia drew the silver out grain by grain, never tearing the mother's flesh wider, never letting the fragments drift near the foals. The metal did not simply obey her hands. It passed through them first, and where it crossed her wrists, thin grey lines rose beneath the skin like frost spreading under glass, then sank back, though not entirely. She held the current in a narrow spiral while her own breath grew shallow and blood began again beneath her nose.  
The mother struck the water with a changing hand.  
Edward endured the blow against his shoulder and remained beside her.  
"Nearly," Amelia said.  
The word broke at the edge.  
The blue current tightened. The silver fragments twisted together above the water, forming a dark, glittering knot no larger than a walnut. Amelia lifted one hand. The knot followed, suspended inside a sphere of water.  
Her arm shook violently.  
"The basin," she said.  
Niall looked around, found an Ashcroft instrument tray overturned beside the ruined machinery, and seized the shallow copper bowl from it. He brought it to Amelia.  
"Not your hands," she warned.  
He held the bowl beneath the sphere.  
Amelia released the spell.  
The water collapsed into the copper with a hard splash. Silver hit bottom in a hiss and a burst of steam. Niall nearly dropped it. Heat traveled through the metal, but he held on until Amelia took a strip of torn leather and laid it over the top.  
"Set it well away from the pool," she said.  
Niall carried the covered basin to the far side of the cavern and placed it upon dry stone. When he turned back, Amelia had both hands in the water again.  
The light had softened.  
It moved over the mother's burns in broad, cooling bands. Angry black edges faded to deep red. Blood stopped clouding the pool. The mother still shook, but the shaking had begun to lose its violence. Her breath lengthened. Her sharp teeth withdrew enough for her mouth to look almost human.  
Amelia bowed over the water.  
For the first time since Niall had known her, she looked not merely tired but fragile.  
He returned to her side. "Your Grace?"  
"Do not interrupt."  
"I was going to offer my arm."  
"Then do it silently."  
He braced himself beside her. Amelia caught his forearm with one wet hand and used the support without looking away from the mother. The touch left a luminous blue print across his sleeve that faded after a breath.  
The mother watched them.  
Some of the hardness eased from her silver gaze.  
Amelia whispered a final word.  
The light went out.  
The absence of it made the cavern seem suddenly colder.  
For several seconds no one moved.  
Then the mother's human form began to dissolve.  
The change was not the violent collapse Niall had witnessed under the nets. It moved with the slow coherence of the tide returning to its proper course. Dark scales spread across her shoulders and down her arms. Her hands lengthened and folded into powerful forelegs. Reed-black hair thickened into a streaming mane. Her spine arched. Her body rose from the water, growing until the woman was gone and the great water-horse stood in her place.  
The wounds remained. Pale burns crossed the midnight blue hide, but they were no longer smoking or bleeding. Her flanks rose and fell with deep, steady breaths.  
She was not healed entirely.  
She was herself again.  
The foals called to her.  
The mother turned at once. She lowered her head and touched each of them with her muzzle, inspecting ears, necks, and trembling legs. The smaller foal pressed beneath her jaw. The other moved to her burned side and stopped, uncertain.  
She nudged it forward.  
Only after the young were beside her did she look back at the Vanguard.  
Edward remained in the water, unarmed. Amelia was still gripping Niall's arm. Niall stood with blood on his face and no knife in his sheath.  
The mother approached Edward. Her hooves made no sound beneath the water.  
She lowered her head until the broad, scaled bridge of her nose rested against his chest.  
Edward lifted one bare hand, but he did not touch her until she leaned closer. Then he laid his palm against the coarse, kelp-tangled mane below her ear.  
"Take them deep," he said. "The mechanisms on the shore will be removed. No iron will remain in your water."  
The mother exhaled against him.  
She drew away and turned toward Amelia.  
For a moment Niall thought she might press her muzzle to the Duchess as she had to Edward. Instead she lowered her head until one silver eye was level with Amelia's face.  
Amelia, still kneeling, inclined her own head.  
"Go," she whispered.  
The mother looked at Niall last.  
He could not read accusation or gratitude in the ancient brightness of her gaze. Perhaps both were human vanities. Perhaps she saw only another armed creature who had chosen, once, to place himself between a hunter and his intended prey.  
Her attention moved to the empty sheath at his belt.  
Then she called to her foals.  
The sound rolled through the cavern like stone singing beneath deep water.  
The young answered. One by one, they slipped toward the black opening at the rear of the pool. Their pale scales caught the torchlight and vanished. The mother waited until the second narrow tail disappeared beneath the surface.  
She looked back once.  
Then the water closed over her mane.  
A dark shape moved below the surface, vast and graceful despite its injuries. It passed beneath the rock arch and entered the submerged channel leading toward the loch. For several heartbeats, a faint silver radiance traced her course through the water.  
Then that too was gone.  
The pool stilled.  
No cage closed behind them. No Vanguard mark was placed upon their territory. Edward gave no order about where they should live, how they should behave, or what debt they owed for their rescue.  
They had been freed.  
They had chosen their own direction.  
The strength went out of Niall's legs.  
He caught himself against the wall, missed, and sank into the wet shale. His body folded with none of the discipline Edward had spent three weeks trying to teach him. The tremor returned all at once, violent enough to knock his teeth together.  
Amelia lowered herself onto a nearby stone rather than pretend she had not seen. Edward retrieved his discarded coat and weapons from the water's edge, checking each by habit. He buckled nothing on yet.  
Niall stared at his hands.  
There was blood beneath his nails that might have belonged to the mercenary he had fought. The heel of his palm had split on the shale. His throwing fingers opened and closed without his permission.  
He had saved Amelia.  
The fact should have felt solid.  
Instead, he recalled the seconds before the throw.  
He remembered being unable to move.  
"I froze," he said.  
Edward looked over.  
Niall's voice sounded small in the cavern. He hated it for that and continued anyway.  
"When we landed. When the rifles started. I saw what was happening, and I could not make my body obey me." He swallowed. "You told me to watch the Duchess's flank. I nearly failed before I had taken two steps."  
Edward crossed the shale and stopped in front of him.  
Niall forced himself to look up.  
The Duke's expression was unreadable. That made the shame worse.  
"I thought it would pass," Niall said. "The fear. I thought there would be a moment when it cleared and I would know what to do. There wasn't. I threw because there was no time left, not because I had mastered anything."  
"No," Edward said. "There rarely is."  
Niall looked down again. "Then I am not what you believed."  
"What do you imagine I believed?"  
"That I was ready."  
"I brought you because you were not."  
The answer struck him hard enough to lift his head.  
Edward crouched before him. He did not soften the words with a smile.  
"Training tells me what you can do when the ground is level, the target is fixed, and no one is trying to kill you. A first mission tells me what remains when none of those things are true."  
Niall's fingers closed into fists. "What remained was fear."  
"Yes."  
There was no reassurance in the word. Only fact.  
Edward reached down and picked up the dagger Niall had dropped when Percival fled. He inspected the edge, wiped it clean on a discarded strip of canvas, and set it on the stone between them.  
"A fool feels no fear because he does not understand the danger," he said. "A coward understands it and chooses himself at the expense of everyone beside him. Terror is neither virtue nor disgrace. It is a warning carried by the body. What matters is the choice made while the warning is sounding."  
Niall thought of his feet fixed to the ground. "I delayed."  
"You did."  
The honesty hurt.  
Edward continued. "Your footing was poor. Your attention narrowed. You allowed the noise to command you for several seconds too long. All of that will be corrected. None of it changed what you did when correction was no longer possible."  
Niall glanced toward Amelia.  
She sat with her head resting briefly against the stone, eyes closed, burned hands open upon her knees.  
"I threw," he said.  
"You threw."  
"I almost missed."  
"Almost is a word for the training yard. In the cave, the ball did not strike my wife." Edward's gaze did not leave him. "Do you believe Alasdair stood before that Ashcroft weapon because he was unafraid?"  
Niall could not answer.  
He had built his father from Edward's sparse accounts, his mother's careful memories, and the few objects that remained: a field compass, a cracked leather journal, two knives worn thin by sharpening. In those fragments Alasdair Fraser had never hesitated. Dead men were easily made fearless because they could no longer contradict the living.  
Edward seemed to read the thought.  
"Your father was afraid," he said. "I know because I saw his face. He understood precisely what would happen if he moved, and he moved anyway. That is why the act matters."  
The cavern blurred.  
Niall blinked hard. "You said he saved your life."  
"He did."  
"And that is why you brought me to Sterling Castle."  
Edward's jaw tightened slightly. "It is why I watched over your family. It is why I made certain you had an education and why I followed the reports of your progress. It is now why you are sitting in this cave."  
Niall gave a bitter, exhausted laugh. "With respect, Your Grace, that distinction has never been as clear to me as it appears to be to you."  
"Then hear it clearly now."  
Edward leaned closer.  
"Alasdair Fraser did not track the Ashcroft party along this shore today. He did not identify the restraint marks. He did not hold his position when Percival offered him vengeance disguised as justice. And he did not throw that knife."  
The Duke's voice dropped.  
"Your father died before he could make any of those choices for you. You made them."  
Niall stared at him.  
Something he had carried since arriving at Sterling Castle shifted. It did not disappear. Alasdair's death remained between them, too large to be resolved by one mission or one speech. Edward's debt remained real. So did Niall's fear that every kindness he received had been purchased with his father's blood.  
But the knife had left Niall's hand.  
No dead man had guided it through the smoke.  
Edward stood and offered him the cleaned dagger, hilt first.  
Niall took it.  
"Thank you, sir," he said.  
"Do not thank me yet. Your performance requires a considerable report."  
Amelia opened one eye. "A very considerable report," she said.  
Niall looked toward her.  
The Duchess raised her head from the wall with visible effort. Her voice remained dry enough to disguise how badly her hands were shaking.  
"Your release was late. Your footing was dreadful. You struck Mr. Higgins higher than intended and abandoned a fine silver blade in his shoulder."  
"Yes, Your Grace."  
"You also ignored the temptation to put a dagger through Lord Percival after the dagger had passed, though I concede your expression suggested a vigorous internal debate."  
"Yes, Your Grace."  
"We shall work on the expression. It reveals far too much."  
Edward's mouth moved at one corner.  
Amelia looked down at her blistered palms, then back at Niall. The wit left her face.  
"You saw a threat I did not," she said. "You acted before he fired, true. You saved my life."  
Niall tried to answer, but nothing came.  
Amelia inclined her head. It was the same brief gesture she had given him across the battle, but this time there was no fire between them.  
"Thank you, Mr. Fraser."  
The words landed more deeply than praise.  
Niall bowed his head. "You're welcome, Your Grace."  
"Good. Now that we have endured sincerity, let us not make a habit of it."  
Edward rose and went to his sword belt. He fastened it around his waist, replaced the hidden blades, and checked the cavern once more: the boundless pool, the ruined apparatus, the unconscious mercenary, the covered basin of poisoned silver.  
Then he returned to Niall.  
"Stand," he said.  
Niall looked at him uncertainly.  
"Mr. Fraser. Stand."  
He planted one boot, then the other. Edward did not pull him up. Niall understood that this too was intentional. He rose under his own strength, swaying once before he found his balance.  
Edward regarded him with the same severity he had shown in the training room, in the study, and on the shore.  
"You entered this cave as a probationary operative of the Shadow Vanguard," he said.  
Niall became very still.  
Amelia watched from the stone.  
"You will leave it as a Vanguard," Edward continued. "Junior, unseasoned, and in need of more instruction than you presently imagine possible. But Vanguard. You have earned the duty and burden attached to it."  
For a moment Niall only heard water.  
He had expected acceptance, if it came at all, to feel like a door opening onto certainty. Instead it felt like standing at the edge of a road whose distance he could not measure.  
There would be more caves. More frightened creatures. More men who called cruelty progress and profit law. There would be moments when fear arrived before thought and moments when anger offered itself as courage.  
The title did not promise he would never fail.  
It required him to choose again.  
Niall straightened despite the pain in his ribs.  
"I understand, Your Grace."  
Edward studied him. "Do you?"  
"No, sir," Niall admitted. "Not fully."  
"Better. Men who believe they understand duty entirely are generally preparing to misuse it."  
Amelia pushed herself to her feet. Niall offered his arm again. This time she accepted without comment.  
Edward retrieved his coat and settled it over her shoulders rather than his own. She gave him a look that promised an argument once she possessed the strength for one, but she drew the fabric closed.  
"We must remove what we can from the shore before the next tide," Edward said. "The restraints will be dismantled. The records and devices go with us. The wounded man will receive treatment and custody in that order."  
Niall glanced toward the dark pool. "And the Kelpies?"  
"Are free," Edward said.  
"Will they remain at the loch?"  
"That is not ours to decide."  
The answer settled the last question the Ashcroft machinery had imposed upon the cave. The institute had spoken of specimens, ownership, transport, and sale. The Vanguard had broken the cage and left the choice where it belonged.  
Edward lifted a torch from the wall and handed it to Niall.  
"Lead us out, Mr. Fraser."  
Niall took it.  
The flame shook with his hand, throwing uncertain light across the passage. He looked once more at the still water where the mother and her young had vanished.  
He thought of Alasdair stepping before a weapon years ago. He thought of the knife crossing the cavern today. The two acts belonged to the same lineage, perhaps, but not because courage passed cleanly from father to son.  
It had to be chosen each time.  
Niall turned toward the tunnel.  
Behind him came Amelia's measured steps and Edward's heavier tread. Ahead waited darkness, cold air, and the long work of undoing what the Ashcroft Institute had left along the shore.  
He was still frightened.  
He went first anyway.

# **Chapter 6**

# The Growing Threat

## Loch Maree, Scottish Highlands & London, England \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

Three days after the fighting, Loch Maree wore innocence like a lie.  
Sunlight broke through the thinning clouds and spread in pale bands across the water. From the eastern shore the loch looked peaceful. Dark pines reflected on its surface, mountains standing blue and distant beyond the mist, gulls wheeling where no birds had dared to call before. Nothing in that calm suggested hidden chains, burned flesh, or children dragged beneath the shallows by machinery built in London.  
The village remembered.  
Black cloth hung from two cottage doors. The men who had carried iron hooks down to the water now kept their eyes lowered when they passed it. The captured winch and restraining frames sat wrapped in canvas within Laird Macrae's disused coach house, sealed at every entrance by Edward's own hand. The silver wire had been drawn out separately, submerged in oil, and locked inside an iron-bound chest so that no frightened villager who found it could mistake it for an ordinary tool.  
Niall stood at a narrow table in the parish room, copying the final list of losses into Edward's field ledger.  
Two dead. Three injured during the search. Six fishing nets destroyed. One crofter's boat split against the rocks. Livestock abandoned while families barricaded themselves indoors. A week's work lost to panic. More damage than Ashcroft would ever trouble himself to record, because none of it could be displayed behind glass or sold by the ounce.  
His right side protested each breath, a strip of linen bound tight where the pike haft had caught him. The heel of his palm had been stitched by the local physician with black thread that pulled whenever he closed his fingers. He had slept twice since leaving the cave. Neither interval had lasted long enough to feel like rest.  
Across the room, Amelia sat with both hands immersed in shallow porcelain basins of cool water. The blisters across her palms had darkened at the edges, and thin silver-gray lines still traced under the skin, where poison drawn from the Kelpie's wounds had entered her through the healing. She had refused laudanum, accepted willow bark with open suspicion, and spent the morning dictating instructions to three different people without once admitting that lifting a teacup caused her pain.  
Edward stood beside the parish clerk, signing orders for food, repairs, and burial expenses.  
The official account was simple enough to survive repetition: illegal hunters had concealed dangerous apparatus in the loch. Two villagers had died after becoming entangled. The Crown had intervened. The surviving offenders had fled and would be pursued through private channels.  
It was not the whole truth.  
It was true enough to prevent another hunt.  
"The Campbells will not accept charity," the clerk said, turning Edward's order over in his hands. He was a slight man, his spectacles seeming perpetually in danger of sliding from his nose. "Morag's father is proud. He will say he can bury his own child."  
"Then it is not charity," Edward replied. "It is compensation for harm caused by an unlawful operation, conducted under the negligence of men who claim influence in London. Call it a Crown indemnity. Pride generally tolerates justice better than it tolerates kindness."  
The clerk looked at the sum again. "This is a great deal of justice, Your Grace."  
"It is a beginning."  
The clerk's glance drifted past Edward to the grey water beyond the window. "The old folk already call her Rhoswen," he said, quiet, half to himself, "whatever name the Crown puts to the record."  
Edward gave no sign of having heard anything in it worth remarking on.  
Edward set another paper beside the first. "The same for Iain Macrae's household. The physician remains a fortnight at my expense. Any family that lost work during the panic receives grain, lamp oil, and winter fodder from the estate stores. You will keep the accounts exact."  
"Yes, Your Grace."  
"And no one enters the northern cave."  
The clerk's expression tightened. "Because of the hunters?"  
"Because the rockfall is unstable, the water passages are dangerous, and curious men have a talent for creating the very disaster they meant only to observe. That should be reason enough."  
It was delivered in the tone Edward used when reasons had ceased to be negotiable.  
The clerk gathered his papers and left.  
Niall finished the last line, sanded the ink, and closed the ledger. His hand shook, only slightly.  
Amelia noticed anyway.  
"If you tear those stitches," she said, "I shall make you explain to the physician why a man entrusted with knives was defeated by a pen."  
"I am being careful, Your Grace."  
"You are being stubborn with admirable concentration. It is not the same thing."  
Niall set the pen down.  
A cart rattled to a stop outside. Voices rose in the lane, then hurried footsteps, then the parish door swinging hard enough to strike the wall.  
Ewan Macrae stood in the doorway.  
He looked cleaner than he had in Edward's study, though no less exhausted. Someone at Sterling Castle had found him a dry coat, replaced his ruined boots, and set a broad-shouldered household agent to watch over him. The man now waited discreetly in the lane. The journey north had put color back into Ewan's face and taken most of the terror out of it, but uncertainty still held him at the threshold.  
His gaze moved first to Edward, then to Amelia's bandaged hands, then to Niall's bruised jaw and bound ribs.  
"Your Graces," he said. "And Mr. Fraser."  
"You were instructed to rest," Edward said.  
Ewan drew himself straighter. "I did, sir. For most of the day."  
Amelia looked at Niall. "Astonishing. We have found someone younger than Mr. Fraser with an even less developed instinct for self-preservation."  
Ewan flushed.  
Edward's expression held severe for another heartbeat before it eased. "Come in."  
Ewan crossed the room and pulled off his cap. His eyes went to the windows where the loch showed between rooftops in a strip of cold blue.  
"Is it dead?" he asked.  
No one pretended not to understand.  
"The Kelpie?" Edward said.  
Ewan nodded.  
"No."  
The boy's fingers tightened around the cap. "Then it killed Morag and Ian?"  
"No. Men killed them. The creature was caught in the same operation and it defended its young."  
Ewan stared at him.  
Niall remembered the mother in the cave, collapsing out of scale and muscle into the shape of an injured woman. He heard again the raw, human plea beneath the chains:  
Not the little ones.  
Ewan looked from Edward to Amelia. "There were young?"  
"Two," Amelia said. "They are alive."  
Something moved across Ewan's face, relief at first, then confusion at feeling relief for the same creatures his neighbors had meant to harm.  
"Where are they now?"  
"Where they chose to go," Edward said.  
That answer seemed to require more thought than Ewan had left in him after the journey. He looked down at his cap.  
"Laird Macrae said I was a fool to ride south. Said the Duke of Norwich would never believe a frightened lad with mud on his coat."  
"Laird Macrae underestimated both of us," Edward said.  
Ewan looked up.  
Edward crossed the room and stopped before him. "You rode through a storm because people were dying, and because no one nearer was willing to ask whether the story they'd chosen was true. Your message reached us in time. Had you delayed another day, the mother and her young would have been taken or killed, and more villagers might have died in the apparatus left behind."  
Ewan's throat worked. "I only carried a letter."  
"Many disasters endure because no one carries the letter."  
Edward held out his hand.  
After a startled pause, Ewan took it.  
The Duke's grip was brief and formal, but Ewan came out of it standing taller.  
"Thank you," Edward said.  
Niall had heard Edward give commands, warnings, judgements, and the restrained approval that passed for praise in the training yard. The plainness of the gratitude carried more weight than ceremony would have.  
Ewan glanced toward Niall, perhaps because one young man was easier to face than a duke. "Did you find the hunters?"  
"We did," Niall said. His ribs ached at the memory.  
"And stopped them?"  
"For now."  
Ewan's attention settled on the linen bound around Niall's palm. "Was it bad?"  
Amelia lifted one hand from the basin, water running between her fingers. "Mr. Fraser's account will improve considerably with each telling. By Christmas he will have defeated twenty men while carrying the Duke under one arm and a rounded horse under the other."  
"Your Grace," Niall said. "I had intended to wait until the new year before adding the horse."  
Ewan laughed before he could stop himself.  
The sound startled the room. It was the first easy sound Niall had heard since arriving at the loch.  
Amelia's mouth curved faintly. Edward returned to the table and lifted a sealed packet.  
"Mr. Macrae. I have another question."  
Ewan sobered. "Yes, Your Grace?"  
"What have you told your family about Sterling Castle?"  
The question set the study's fire between them again, though there was none in the room.  
Ewan's eyes flicked once toward Amelia's hands. "That I delivered Laird Macrae's letter. That the Crown believed the deaths were caused by poachers using traps. That you came north to investigate."  
"Nothing else?"  
"No, sir."  
"Not the manner in which the hearth lit?"  
Ewan's ears reddened. "It was a draft down the chimney. Her Grace said so herself."  
Amelia tilted her head with approval. "At last. A witness who respects expert testimony."  
Edward studied Ewan for several seconds.  
"Discretion is not silence for its own sake," he said. "It is knowing which truth belongs to which listener. Your village needed to know the creature was not to blame, and that the devices were human-made. It did not need explanations that would send collectors, priests, journalists, and frightened men with guns into the hills."  
"I understand."  
"Do you?"  
Ewan considered the question rather than answering it quickly. "I understand enough not to make it worse."  
Edward gave a small nod. "That is often the most useful understanding to have."  
He placed the sealed packet in Ewan's hands.  
"This contains instructions for Laird Macrae, regarding the compensation funds and the closure of the northern cave. You will deliver it in person. If questions arise, he may write to my private office rather than to a public magistrate."  
Ewan turned the packet over. The Sterling seal shone dark red in the morning light.  
"There may be other letters in the future," Edward went on. "Matters of estate business, local disturbances, unusual travelers, or reports best carried by someone who does not embellish what he has seen. The work would be occasional, and paid. You would not be asked to investigate, or fight, or place yourself in danger. Only to carry correspondence, and to notice the ordinary details of the road."  
Niall heard the limits Edward had built into the offer. Ewan was being trusted, not initiated. The distinction spared him a burden he had never asked to carry.  
"For the Crown?" Ewan asked.  
"When necessary. For my household, at other times."  
Ewan looked at the seal again. "Why me?"  
Edward's gaze moved briefly toward Niall.  
The question had been asked once already, in another room, in another storm.  
"Because you have already shown that distance does not stop you from acting, when action is required," Edward said. "Because you notice more than fear allows most people to notice. And because when you met something you did not understand, you carried the problem to those who might."  
Ewan absorbed this in silence.  
"You may refuse," Edward added. "The gratitude remains regardless."  
The boy closed his fingers around the packet. "I will carry them."  
Edward drew a second item from his coat, smaller than the first: a plain packet bearing no address, tied with a length of black cord rather than sealed.  
"This one carries no household name, and no instruction you are meant to read until it is needed. If three knocks come to your door after dark, evenly spaced, on a night when no one has sent for you, open it then. Not before."  
Ewan turned the blank parcel over in his hands. "And if they never come?"  
"Then you will have carried it for nothing, and I will consider that the better outcome."  
"Then begin with that one."  
Ewan put on his cap, remembered where he stood, and took it off again.  
Niall looked down quickly, so the smile would not be mistaken for mockery.  
When Ewan had gone, the room fell quiet again.  
"He will make a good courier," Amelia said, as she drew her hands from the basins. The water around them had taken on a faint gray cast.  
"If he learns to sleep," Edward replied.  
"We have failed entirely to teach Mr. Fraser that lesson. I see little cause for confidence."  
Niall closed the field ledger. "I slept, on the journey north."  
"You lost consciousness against a carriage wall. The distinction is medical."  
Edward gathered the remaining papers. "The last of the apparatus leaves for secure storage at noon. We depart when the wagons move."  
Niall looked through the window toward the loch. No dark shape broke the surface. He had caught himself searching for one every time he passed a window since they'd left the cave, though he could not decide whether he hoped to see her, or hoped she had gone somewhere no human eye could follow.  
"Do you believe the Ashcrofts will return?" he asked.  
Edward tied the papers with cord. "Not today."  
It was not an answer meant to comfort him.

## The Scottish Highlands

The road away from Loch Maree climbed through wet heather and folded into the mountains.  
Amelia traveled inside the hired carriage, her burned hands wrapped and resting on a cushion. The physician had prescribed quiet. Amelia had informed him that quiet was not a treatment, and the argument held for perhaps the length of one mile before the rhythm of the wheels and the willow draught settled the matter for her.  
Edward and Niall rode alongside.  
The recovered Ashcroft equipment followed in two guarded wagons behind them: dismantled winch gears, galvanic cells packed in straw, ledgers sealed against the rain, lengths of silver-threaded restraint mesh, and the walking stick Percival had abandoned in the cave. Every object had been catalogued. Every stain had been preserved. The Institute might operate beyond the reach of public law, but Edward would not let secrecy excuse carelessness.  
Niall's gelding picked its way around a washout, and the motion pulled at his ribs.  
"You are learning," Edward said.  
"The saddle is uneven."  
"The saddle has not changed since morning."  
Niall straightened, and regretted it at once.  
Edward allowed him several minutes of silence before he spoke again.  
"You asked, in the cave, whether Alasdair's death was the reason you came to Sterling Castle."  
Niall kept his eyes on the road. "I remember."  
"I did not answer as completely as I should have."  
That alone was enough to make him look over.  
Edward rode with one hand on the reins, his injured forearm bound beneath the sleeve of his coat. Behind him, the mountains were already dissolving into mist.  
"When your father died," he said, "I promised him nothing. There was no time for it. He could not speak, and I had no right to turn his last moments into an agreement he had no way to accept."  
Niall said nothing.  
"The promise was mine," Edward went on. "I made it afterward. I would see his family was not abandoned for what he had chosen to do for me. That meant providing for you, not owning you."  
The phrase struck Niall because it echoed the cave's lesson exactly. Protection was not possession. A debt could become its own kind of cage, if the man paying it decided what the payment had bought him.  
"You followed my education," Niall said.  
"Yes."  
"You sent the books."  
"Some of them. Your mother refused money when it was offered outright. Books offended her pride rather less."  
A reluctant warmth moved through him. That sounded precisely like her.  
"And when my reports became inconveniently good?" he asked.  
"They became interesting."  
"That is nearly praise," Niall said, still looking straight ahead.  
"Do not grow accustomed to it," the Duke said, the corner of his lip turning up ever so slightly.  
For a few yards only the hooves and the wagon wheels spoke.  
Edward looked ahead. "My promise opened a door, Niall. It did not carry you through it. Your knowledge, your discipline, your accuracy, those earned you the offer. What you did at the loch earned you active standing. What comes after will be earned again, every time it matters."  
"And if I fail?" Niall said, rubbing his thumb over the bandage at his palm.  
"You will." The Duke's answer came without a breath's hesitation.  
Niall turned in the saddle.  
Edward's face stayed composed. "You will misjudge a trail. Trust the wrong account. Arrive too late. Or discover that the right decision still carries a cost you cannot repair. Every operative fails, Niall. The danger lies in believing that your father's name, or my favor, or one good throw exempts you from that truth."  
Niall let out a breath. "You have a remarkable gift for making advancement sound like a sentence."  
"It is a duty. Sentences end, eventually."  
Despite himself, Niall smiled. It faded when he looked back at the covered wagons. "Percival knows who I am now."  
"He suspects. By the time he reaches London, he will know."  
"Does that concern you?"  
"Yes."  
Again, no comforting lie offered in its place.  
"Then why name my father before him?"  
Edward's jaw tightened. "Because Silas Ashcroft has spent years treating Alasdair's death as a closed account. It is not. I wanted him to understand that the men he kills do not vanish simply because he has filed away their names."  
"You used me to send that message."  
"I did."  
Niall felt the first edge of anger rise, and then found it dulled, somehow, by Edward's refusal to dress the truth up as anything gentler.  
"Was that fair?"  
"No," Edward said. "It was necessary, and I judged you could bear it. Those are not the same thing."  
The road narrowed between two slopes, and Niall's horse drifted closer to Edward's.  
"I do not want to spend my life as the consequence of my father's death," Niall said.  
"Nor should you," Edward said, looking at him fully at last.  
"But that is what the Ashcrofts will see. Another Fraser standing beside another Sterling."  
"Let them begin with that mistake," Edward said, his voice dropping. "Honoring Alasdair does not require you to become him. It requires me to let you become your own man, even when that man chooses a path I would rather he had been spared."  
Niall looked away before his face could give away more than he meant it to.  
Below them, the Loch had narrowed to a strip between the hills. Sunlight caught the water once, and vanished.  
"He would have hated my landing," Niall said.  
"Profoundly."  
"Did he ever freeze?"  
"Often enough to know it in others."  
"Did he ever miss?"  
Edward considered the question. "He would insist the target had moved."  
Niall laughed, and the pain in his ribs made him stop.  
Edward's mouth shifted at one corner.  
The road bent south, and Loch Maree disappeared behind the mountain.

## London, England

London received Percival Ashcroft under a ceiling of coal smoke.  
Rain had polished the black railings outside the Institute's Mayfair house and turned its streetlamps into blurred yellow wounds in the fog. The building wore a respectable face for the square: pale stone, curtained windows, a brass plate engraved "Ashcroft Institute For Natural Inquiry." Members of Parliament had attended lectures there. Physicians had praised its collections. Men who would have recoiled from an ordinary public butcher's yard walked through its doors gladly, to discuss specimens over port.  
Behind the public galleries, the Institute kept its true accounts.  
Percival crossed the private upper corridor with one hand pressed to the bandage at his throat. Every step pulled at the bruising along his back. His ruined coat had been replaced before he ever reached London, but the new collar could not hide the thin cut Duke Edward Sterling's blade had left below his jaw.  
Servants nervously looked away as he passed.  
At the end of the corridor, two iron doors stood open which led into Silas Ashcroft's private laboratory.  
The room ran warmer than the rest of the house. Gas jets hissed behind wire guards. Glass cases climbed floor to ceiling, holding preserved wings, horns, bones, skins, and organs suspended in amber fluid. A row of experimental galvanic cells occupied one wall, copper and zinc plates each labeled in a meticulous hand, fine wires running from them to a restraint collar fixed around a carved wooden throat.  
At the center of the room, Lord Silas Ashcroft wrote in a ledger with a steel-nibbed pen. He did not look up when Percival entered.  
"A mother and two viable young," Silas said. "A transport frame built to your own specifications. Eleven hired men, one field clerk, four galvanic batteries. Nearly two hundred yards of silver-threaded mesh."  
The pen continued its measured scratching.  
"Yes, Father."  
"And you returned with no specimens, no equipment, half your party injured, and a physician's invoice from York describing a knife wound, burns, fractures, and an improbable quantity of water in the lungs."  
Percival's jaw tightened. "Sterling ambushed us."  
The pen stopped.  
Silas blotted the line, set the pen beside the ledger, and raised his eyes. He was not an imposing man, not in the manner of Edward Sterling. His power lay in economy: white hair combed precisely back from a narrow face, pale eyes that rested on every object as though pricing its component parts, hands too clean to suggest they had ever touched the work displayed around them.  
"Sterling did not ambush you," he said. "He investigated evidence you failed to conceal. There is a difference, and it is the difference between misfortune and incompetence."  
Heat climbed beneath Percival's collar. "Two villagers entered the restraints unexpectedly. Their deaths accelerated local suspicion."  
"Your report called them acceptable losses."  
"They were."  
"Then acceptable losses should not have cost you the operation, and us the specimens," Silas said, closing the ledger.  
"Tell me exactly what happened. Not what you wished had happened. Not what your surviving men are prepared to swear to. And not whatever will sound least humiliating once it's reduced to paper."  
Percival began with the cave. He described Edward descending from the ledge with a sword already bare. Amelia's fire crossing the chamber. The restraint chains breaking. The mother losing her equine form and speaking, like a woman. The young recruit freezing amid the rifle fire, then throwing with enough accuracy to put Higgins down across the cavern.  
He left out the sound he himself made when Edward's blade found his throat.  
Silas noticed omissions the way other men noticed blood. "Sterling captured you," he said.  
"Briefly."  
"And released you."  
"He threatened the Institute."  
"Those two statements are not incompatible. What were his precise words?"  
Percival repeated them with visible reluctance, the warning against returning to protected territory, the confiscation of the equipment, and the final message concerning Alasdair Fraser.  
At the name, Silas went still.  
"Fraser," he said.  
"The young operative. Nineteen, perhaps. Red-haired. Scottish." Percival's jaw worked. "I had no name for him in the cave. I have one now. The household calls him Mr. Fraser."  
Silas rose and crossed to a cabinet of narrow drawers. He drew one open without reading the labels and removed a file bound in faded green ribbon.  
Percival recognized the hand on the cover as belonging to the Institute's former field director.  
Silas opened it.  
Inside lay an old survey map, two reports, and a charcoal likeness of a broad-shouldered man with light hair and watchful eyes. Below the portrait, someone had written: 'Alasdair Fraser \- Tracker//Vanguard Affiliate.' A second notation had been added in darker ink: 'Deceased During Recovery Failure.'  
Percival stepped closer. "You knew him?"  
"I knew of him. He disrupted three operations in the north, and removed more property from Institute custody than most men manage in a lifetime."  
"Sterling said we killed him."  
"We did."  
Silas spoke with no more feeling than if he were confirming the disposal of a broken instrument.  
Percival studied the portrait again. "The son resembles him."  
"That is rarely the important inheritance."  
Silas turned to the concluding report, his fingers moving down the page. "Alasdair Fraser died protecting Edward Sterling during a failed transfer. The family remained in Scotland. A widow. One male child."  
He looked up. "Sterling has brought the son into his household."  
"As an operative?"  
"As an investment."  
Silas returned the report to its file and carried it to his desk.  
"Edward has always mistaken continuity for virtue. He believes that if one generation goes on protecting what the last one protected, the cause acquires some moral weight of its own. A Sterling commands. A Fraser stands at his shoulder. Old names, repeating old errors, until the repetition is mistaken for a kind of law."  
Percival touched the bandage at his throat. "The younger Fraser can be removed. He is untested. Without Sterling and the witch beside him, he would have died in that cave."  
Silas's gaze sharpened. "And yet, he did not."  
"He froze."  
"Then he acted. Men who act without fear are predictable. Men who act through it are considerably less so. Do not dismiss him merely because you happened to witness the moment before he became dangerous."  
Percival's irritation hardened. "What would you have me do? Congratulate him?"  
"Learn from the failure you purchased at such expense."  
Silas moved to the window. Beyond the glass, carriages passed like shadows through the fog.  
"The Vanguard responded more quickly than our estimates allowed," he said. "Amelia Sterling's field capacity extends every measurement in our files. Edward remains willing to use Crown authority without ever exposing the mechanism that grants it. And he is recruiting younger operatives from families already hostile to us."  
"Then we strike before they are prepared."  
"At whom?"  
"Sterling. Fraser. The household."  
Silas turned from the window.  
"You have just lost twelve men attempting to overpower one wounded mother in a cave. Your answer is to assault a fortified estate occupied by two senior Vanguard commanders, armed staff, concealed defenses, and whatever else the Sterlings have accumulated beneath their stones."  
Percival said nothing.  
"That is not strategy," Silas went on. "It is embarrassment, looking for a weapon to hold."  
The words landed more cleanly than Edward's blade had.  
Percival forced his voice level. "Sterling has a son."  
Silas's expression did not change, though the silence that followed grew attentive.  
"Alexander James Sterling," Percival said. "Sixteen. The future Duke of Norwich. If Vanguard command passes through the family…"  
Silas walked back to the desk and opened a different drawer. From it he drew a slim social register, a packet of newspaper clippings, and a recent portrait engraved for some charitable subscription. Edward and Amelia stood behind their children. Catherine, younger and bright-eyed, sat beside her brother. Alexander faced the artist with the controlled expression of a boy already being taught that the world would study every failure in his posture.  
Silas set one finger upon the heir's image.  
"A weakness attacked becomes a defended point," he said. "A child threatened is hidden away, guarded, taught to hate the hand that reached for him. Edward would close every door before we ever learned where any of them led."  
"Then what?"  
"We observe."  
Percival frowned.  
Silas began arranging papers across the desk.  
"The Sterling estate employs hundreds directly and touches thousands more through its leases, its suppliers, its charities, its offices, its patronage. No fortress stays sealed once it must purchase coal, receive post, hire servants, entertain officials, repair its roofs, and send its heir out into society."  
He selected a blank sheet.  
"We will learn which antiquarians correspond with Edward's private office. Which servants travel unexpectedly. Which physicians are summoned after supposed hunting accidents. Which estates receive sealed crates. Which names recur beside Sterling operations."  
The steel nib touched ink.  
"We will cultivate men in Parliament who resent private Crown authority. We will find scholars willing to argue that unusual creatures, if they exist at all, are resources of national importance rather than the property of hereditary wardens. We will plant questions in committees, journals, museums, drawing rooms. Not accusations: questions. Accusations make enemies. Questions grant permission."  
Percival watched the list take shape.  
"And the heir?"  
"We learn him, before we ever move against him. His habits. His friendships. His vanities. His resentments. Whether his parents have initiated him, or merely prepared the ground and left him standing on it."  
Silas tapped the engraving.  
"At sixteen he is old enough to notice secrecy, and young enough to mistake the explanation for the whole of it. If Edward has kept him ignorant, that ignorance can become distrust. If he has told him, duty can become pressure. Either condition may prove useful."  
"You intend to turn him against his family?"  
"I intend to discover whether he can be turned. There is a difference."  
Silas glanced toward Alasdair Fraser's file.  
"The same applies to the young Scot. A son brought into service under the weight of a dead father may prove loyal. He may equally prove angry, indebted, ambitious, or desperate to prove the appointment was earned rather than given out of guilt. Every virtue carries its own fracture, once the correct pressure is applied."  
Percival remembered Niall advancing through the smoke with murder written plainly on his face, and stopping only because Edward spoke a single word.  
"He obeys Sterling."  
"For now."  
"And if observation reveals no fracture?"  
Silas sanded the first page of instructions.  
"Then we create pressure elsewhere. Licenses. Land disputes. Public scrutiny. Rival claims. Interrupted supply. Competing loyalties. Institutions are rarely destroyed by gunfire alone. More often they are simply worn down. Good men spend all their strength answering a hundred small attacks."  
The gas jets hissed. Somewhere behind the wall, a galvanic relay clicked at measured intervals.  
Percival looked around at the cabinets holding every preserved wing, every bottled organ, every bone labeled and numbered in its turn. The Institute had been built on patience as much as on violence. Hunters filled its cases. Clerks, patrons, legislators, and collectors ensured that no single defeat ever closed the market.  
"And Loch Maree?" he asked.  
"Abandoned, for the present. Sterling will expect retaliation there. Expectation is a kind of wall. We shall go where he is not looking."  
Silas folded the instructions and sealed them with black wax.  
"Have Higgins questioned once the physician permits it. I want the exact distance of the Fraser boy's throw, the timing of Amelia's working, and every command Edward gave in that cave. Send copies of the confiscated equipment designs to the laboratory in Manchester. The next restraints will not depend on exposed chains."  
Percival took the packet.  
"You are not to approach Sterling Castle," Silas added. "You are not to threaten the son, bribe a household servant without authorization, or attempt to recover the Loch Maree specimens. Your name is now attached to this failure. For a time, visibility makes you a liability."  
Percival's hand tightened around the seal. "How long?"  
"Until usefulness exceeds pride."  
Silas returned Alasdair's file to its cabinet, but kept the Sterling engraving on his desk.  
The family stared upward from the paper, preserved in ink's false permanence: the commander, the witch, the daughter, and the uninitiated heir who would one day inherit whatever his parents had concealed behind stone and oath.  
Silas lowered the glass shade over the nearest gas flame.  
The room darkened by degrees, leaving the cases to shine in narrow reflections of themselves.  
"Let Edward enjoy his victory," he said. "Let him train his Fraser, and shelter his creatures. Let him believe the old arrangement remains secure, because it survived another night."  
His fingers came to rest on Alexander Sterling's engraved shoulder.  
"Edward Sterling cannot guard his inheritance forever."

# **Chapter 7**

# The First Throw

## Sterling Castle, Norfolk \- Autumn 1845

## \*\*\*

Sterling Castle appeared out of the morning mist one tower at a time. First came the western battlements, dark against a whitening sky. Then the great square keep rose above the trees, followed by the chapel spire and the long eastern range, every roof slick with the rain that had crossed Norfolk during the night. The castle stood upon its plateau as it had for centuries, its walls rooted into the stone, its windows catching the first weak light over the protected forest below. To Alexander Sterling, watching from the upper gallery, it had never looked less impenetrable.  
The carriage approaching through the outer gate carried no crest.  
That alone was enough to hold his attention. His father's vehicles displayed the Sterling arms even when traveling on private business. The plain black carriage below had mud to the lamps, a cracked panel near the rear wheel, and one leather strap bound around the wood where a brass fitting had torn loose. Behind it came a covered wagon under escort by four men Alexander recognized as estate guards, though all wore ordinary traveling coats rather than livery.  
The wagon's canvas had been drawn tight and tied at every corner. Even so, several shapes pressed against it from beneath: the squared edge of a heavy frame, the curve of a wheel or drum, the blackened teeth of some machinery too large to conceal completely. One of the guards rode beside the load with a gloved hand resting near the knot of a tarred rope.  
Alexander leaned closer to the glass.  
The carriage stopped beneath the western arch. Mr. O'Brien, the castle steward, was already waiting in the courtyard with two footmen, the stable master, and three men from his father's private staff. None of the household servants had been permitted near the outer yard, and the great doors closed behind the wagon before anyone began unloading it.  
His father stepped down first.  
Edward Sterling moved with his usual upright control, but his right forearm was bound beneath the sleeve of his coat, and he used his left hand to close the carriage door. Mud had dried in the seams of his boots. There was a dark mark near one shoulder where rain had carried old blood into the wool and left it there.  
Alexander's fingers tightened on the stone window ledge.  
His mother emerged after his father. Amelia accepted Edward's hand, though she usually treated assistance from a carriage as an ornamental custom rather than a necessity. Both her hands were wrapped in white linen from wrist to fingertips. Her face was composed, but the journey had drawn every trace of color from it. She paused once on the step, eyes closed, before placing her feet upon the cobbles.  
The third passenger was the young Scotsman from the private offices.  
Alexander had seen him only at a distance: a head of bright ginger hair bent over a ledger, a tall figure crossing the lower court with a leather dispatch case, an unfamiliar rider in the park who sat a horse better than most clerks. Mr. O'Brien had said the newcomer was a junior assistant engaged for the Duke's antiquarian correspondence. That explanation had been delivered in the steward's driest voice, which generally meant that further questions would produce nothing useful.  
The assistant looked like a man returning from a study of old manuscripts.  
A fading bruise darkened his jaw. One hand was bandaged beneath a fingerless glove, and he held himself carefully as he descended, protecting one side of his ribs. His coat was clean enough to have been changed during the journey, but the boots were still scarred with pale lines where stone had cut leather.  
Exhaustion showed in every line of him.  
His gaze went to the covered wagon before it settled on the castle.  
Edward spoke to Mr. O'Brien. The steward listened without visible reaction, then directed the footmen toward the carriage and the private staff toward the wagon. No one loosened the canvas in the open court. The load was taken through the western service passage, toward the older storehouses and the restricted rooms beneath the keep.  
Alexander had lived at Sterling Castle for his whole sixteen years. He knew every public stair, most of the servants' passages, three ways onto the roof, and at least six doors his parents preferred him not to open.  
He did not know where that wagon was going.  
Nor did he know why his parents had left during a midnight storm four days earlier with a Scottish messenger and returned looking as though they had survived a siege.  
He was already moving before the carriage door closed.  
The upper gallery opened onto a stone stair that curved down through the western tower. Alexander took the steps two at a time until training and dignity reminded him that the future Duke of Norwich was not meant to break his neck because his parents had returned from confidential business. He slowed before the final landing, straightened his waistcoat, and entered the lower hall at a measured pace that fooled no one who knew him.  
Mrs. Thompson emerged from the kitchen passage carrying a tray of broth, bread, and a silver pot large enough to supply a regiment.  
"They're home," Alexander said.  
The cook shifted the tray against one hip. "So the whole castle has discovered. Master Alexander. Bells, boots, horses, and Mr. O'Brien looking as cheerful as a burial. Stand aside unless you mean to carry something."  
He moved, then turned after her. "What happened?"  
"I cook the meals, love. I don't interrogate dukes before breakfast."  
"You interrogate everyone before breakfast."  
"Only people whose answers interest me."  
She continued toward the small morning room used by the family when they wished to avoid ceremony. Alexander followed at a safer distance.  
The door stood open. Edward was removing his traveling coat while a valet attempted, unsuccessfully, to persuade him to sit. Amelia had already taken the chair nearest the fire. A maid knelt beside her with a basin, but Amelia dismissed her with a quiet word before the bandages could be touched.  
The young assistant remained near the door, as though uncertain whether he belonged inside the room or beyond it. He carried two leather cases and a narrow wooden box secured with brass catches. When he saw Alexander enter, he straightened despite the pain the movement caused him.  
Edward looked up.  
For an instant, relief crossed his face before discipline restored the familiar calm.  
"Alexander."  
"Father. Mother."  
Alexander went first to Amelia. He bent and kissed her cheek, careful not to touch her hands.  
The skin at her temple felt cold.  
"You look dreadful," he said.  
"Your devotion remains eloquent," Amelia replied. "I have crossed half of Britain to be insulted before tea."  
"You would be more offended if I pretended not to notice."  
"That is unfortunately true."  
He turned to Edward. His father accepted the brief embrace with his uninjured arm, holding Alexander's shoulder for half a breath longer than usual.  
"What happened?" Alexander asked.  
Edward's gaze shifted once toward the open doorway. Mr. O'Brien appeared there as if summoned by thought.  
"The private breakfast, sir," the steward said.  
"In twenty minutes. No one enters the western store passage without my written order. The covered wagon is to remain sealed until I inspect the inventory."  
"Yes, Your Grace."  
"Send word to Dr. Hale that the Duchess will permit him to examine her hands."  
Amelia looked at her husband. "Will I?"  
"You will."  
"How tyrannical."  
"Mr. Fraser is also to be examined."  
The assistant opened his mouth.  
Edward looked at him.  
He closed it again.  
"An efficient morning," Amelia observed. "Three patients subdued with one expression."  
Mr. O'Brien withdrew, shutting the door behind him.  
Alexander's attention returned to the assistant. Mr. Fraser. The name suited the accent he had heard once in the records room, though not the quiet alertness with which the young man watched every exit.  
Edward followed his gaze.  
"Alexander, this is Mr. Niall Fraser, my new junior assistant. He works with my private secretariat and assists with certain antiquarian matters. Mr. Fraser, my son, Alexander Sterling."  
Niall transferred both cases into his left arm and bowed.  
"Master Alexander."  
His Scottish burr was soft, nearer Edinburgh than the Highlands, but exhaustion had deepened it.  
"Mr. Fraser," Alexander said.  
They regarded one another with the caution of two people who had already formed impressions at a distance and were now forced to test them.  
Niall's expression was respectful without being vacant. Most men newly introduced to the Duke's heir either stared too long or looked away too quickly. Niall did neither. His blue eyes took in Alexander's face, clothing, and posture with the same practical attention he had given the wagon in the courtyard.  
Then the moment passed. Niall set the cases upon a side table.  
"You have not answered me," Alexander said to his father. "What happened?"  
Edward moved to the fire and stood with his back to it. He had always preferred to give difficult explanations from his feet.  
"A messenger brought word of deaths near Loch Maree. Illegal hunters had placed a dangerous restraint apparatus in the water and concealed it from the local people. Two villagers were killed."  
The answer was plain, severe, and incomplete.  
"Hunters of what?"  
"Wildlife and anything else they believed they could sell."  
"Ordinary poachers do not build machinery that requires a covered wagon and four guards."  
Niall's eyes flicked toward him. It was not surprise exactly. More like recognition.  
Edward's face remained still. "These were not ordinary poachers. They were well financed, heavily armed, and willing to let innocent people die rather than expose their operation. The matter falls under confidential Crown authority."  
"And you fought them."  
"They resisted arrest."  
Amelia lifted one bandaged hand a fraction above the chair arm. "Your father is attempting to make a cave full of rifles sound like an administrative disagreement."  
Edward gave her a warning glance.  
Alexander looked at the dried blood near his father's shoulder, then at Niall's jaw and guarded ribs.  
"How many?"  
"Enough."  
"Were you shot?"  
"No."  
"Mother?"  
"Also no."  
"Then what happened to your hands?"  
Amelia's gaze softened, but only slightly. "I handled something dangerous while preventing further harm. The physician will tell me how foolish that was in language less elegant than my own."  
She did not lie. Neither did she answer.  
Alexander knew the shape of this conversation. He had encountered it since childhood whenever his parents returned from diplomatic visits, archaeological journeys, or private work for the Crown: facts arranged like stones across a doorway, each one true and none allowing him through.  
He turned to Niall.  
"And Mr. Fraser? Antiquarian correspondence became violent?"  
A faint color rose above Niall's collar. Edward answered before he could.  
"Mr. Fraser was included because his knowledge of the region proved useful. He tracked the hunters' movements and assisted during the confrontation."  
"Assisted how?"  
Edward's mouth almost formed a smile.  
"An armed man found a position above your mother while her attention was elsewhere. Mr. Fraser struck him across the cavern with a thrown knife before he could fire accurately."  
The whole of Alexander's attention swung to him.  
Niall looked as though he would rather face another cavern than the interest now fixed upon him.  
"Across a cavern," Alexander repeated.  
"The distance has improved considerably since we left Scotland," Niall said. "By London it will have become half a mile."  
Amelia settled deeper into the chair. "Thirty-one yards, according to Mr. Fraser's own irritatingly exact report. Smoke obscured the line. His footing was poor. The blade struck too high."  
"It moved the rifle," Edward said.  
"It did."  
"And saved your life," Alexander said.  
Amelia's eyes moved to Niall.  
"Yes."  
The single word changed the room.  
Niall lowered his gaze, not with false modesty but with visible discomfort at being made the center of the fact.  
Alexander studied the bandaged hand. "Will you teach me?"  
Niall looked up. "Teach you what, sir?"  
"To throw a knife."  
The answer came at once. "No."  
Silence followed.  
Alexander was not accustomed to hearing the world delivered without apology, circumlocution, or a hurried glance toward his father.  
Niall seemed to realize the speed of his refusal only after it had escaped him. He straightened.  
"That is, beg pardon, Master Alexander. I meant that I cannot place a live blade in your hand without His Grace's permission. Nor should anyone teach the heir to the estate a dangerous skill while half asleep and stitched together."  
"I did not ask for a lesson this instant."  
"That improves the proposal somewhat."  
Edward folded his arms.  
"Practice blades," he said. "The balanced iron set from the lower training room. No silver, no sharpened edges, and no throwing toward a target unless the yard is cleared."  
Niall glanced at him. "Your Grace?"  
"Tomorrow afternoon, after Dr. Hale has confirmed that you can raise your arm without reopening anything. One hour to begin."  
Alexander's satisfaction was immediate. "Thank you."  
"You may reserve gratitude until you discover how repetitive proper instruction is," Edward said. "Mr. Fraser will decide whether you are fit to continue after the first hour."  
Niall's expression suggested that this arrangement had advanced more quickly than he preferred.  
"Yes, Your Grace."  
"And Alexander."  
"Father?"  
"You will follow every safety instruction the first time it is given. Mr. Fraser is not one of your fencing companions. He will not allow you to win because your name is above the door."  
Alexander glanced at Niall.  
The Scotsman's face remained properly neutral, but something in his eyes had sharpened with interest.  
"Good," Alexander said.  
A knock sounded. Dr. Hale entered without waiting for permission, carrying a leather case and the expression of a man who had been told three aristocrats intended to resist medical judgement before breakfast.  
Amelia sighed.  
"The Crown sends us hunters," she said. "The household sends us physicians. I know which enemy I fear more."  
Edward pointed toward the chair opposite her.  
"Sit, Niall."  
Niall obeyed with less reluctance than Amelia and considerably more pain than he intended anyone to see.  
Alexander remained until the first bandage was cut away. Beneath the linen, the skin across Amelia's palms was blistered and marked by thin gray lines that vanished beneath her sleeves. Dr. Hale drew a sharp breath. Edward's expression hardened. Niall looked away only after confirming that the physician had everything he required.  
Whatever had happened in Scotland, it was not contained in his father's careful explanation.  
Alexander knew better than to ask again while the walls were raised.  
He also knew walls could be walked around.  
As he left the morning room, he glanced once toward the western passage where the covered machinery had disappeared.  
Then he thought of a silver blade crossing smoke and distance in a stranger's hand.  
For the first time that morning, curiosity outweighed anger.

The following afternoon was clear, cold, and bright enough to reveal every imperfection in the lower training yard.  
Rain had darkened the packed earth and left narrow pools beside the stone wall. The servants had moved the archery butts beneath cover, and someone, probably Mr. O'Brien, acting upon Edward's instruction, had placed a round wooden target against the northern wall where no missed throw could reach a window, horse, or unsuspecting gardener.  
Alexander arrived ten minutes early.  
He had changed out his formal coat into a plain brown waistcoat and an older linen shirt, the sleeves rolled nearly to his forearms. His fencing boots were polished but practical. He wore no cravat. If Mr. Fraser intended to dismiss him as an idle nobleman seeking an afternoon diversion, Alexander meant to give him as little evidence as possible.  
Niall was already there.  
He stood beside a narrow table upon which six practice knives had been arranged in a straight line. They were iron, dull-edged, and slightly heavier through the hilt than a fighting blade. A chalk line marked the throwing position twelve feet from the target.  
Niall wore no coat. His shirt had been left loose at the throat, and a broad strip of linen still bound his ribs beneath it. The bruise along his jaw had turned from purple to yellow at the edges. One palm was wrapped, but the fingers moved freely as he tested the balance of a practice knife.  
He did not notice Alexander immediately.  
The knife rolled over his knuckles, turned at the base of his fingers, and settled into his grip without a glance. It was the sort of movement that came from years of repetition rather than display.  
Alexander stepped onto the gravel.  
Niall caught the blade by the hilt and looked over. "Master Alexander."  
"Mr. Fraser."  
Niall glanced toward the yard clock. "You're early."  
"So are you."  
"I am responsible for ensuring nothing here kills you. It seemed prudent to begin before you arrived."  
"My father exaggerates my fragility."  
"Your father gave me three separate instructions concerning your fingers. I would say he assesses it with great care."  
Alexander approached the table.  
The practice knives seemed unimpressive beside a fencing foil or dueling pistol. Each was no longer than his hand and forearm together, with a broad, blunt point and leather wrapped around the grip. One bore old nicks from repeated strikes against stone.  
He reached for it.  
"Stop."  
Alexander's hand halted above the blade.  
Niall did not raise his voice. He simply waited until Alexander looked at him.  
"First instruction," Niall said. "Do not touch a weapon until you are told it is safe to do so. A dull point can still break a toe, blind a man, or teach a horse to hate you."  
Alexander withdrew his hand. "Very well."  
Niall studied him for a heartbeat, perhaps waiting for irritation.  
When none came, he nodded.  
"The yard has been cleared. The stable passage is barred. Mr. O'Brien has threatened the kitchen boys with consequences I was not invited to hear. We throw only north. If I say stop, you stop. If I say drop it, you open your hand and let it fall. You do not catch a blade by the point, even a blunt one, and you do not turn with a knife in your hand to answer someone behind you."  
"Understood."  
"Say it back."  
Alexander did.  
Niall gestured to the first knife. "Now you may take it. By the hilt."  
Alexander picked it up. The balance surprised him. A fencing weapon announced its direction through the line of the blade. The throwing knife seemed willing to move either way.  
"How does it feel?" Niall asked.  
"Heavy."  
"It is."  
"I expected you to say the weight is an illusion caused by poor grip."  
"No. The weight is iron. Poor grip causes other problems."  
Alexander's mouth twitched.  
Niall took a second knife in his unbandaged hand. "Stand with your left foot forward. Not as far as a fencing lunge. You're not trying to stab the target from here."  
Alexander adjusted.  
"Too wide."  
He narrowed his stance.  
"Your shoulders are turned away. Square them slightly."  
"That leaves the body open."  
"To an opponent, yes. The target is not going to counterattack."  
"One hopes."  
Niall studied the painted circle upon the wood. "If it does, I shall revise the lesson."  
There it was: humor delivered without ceremony, so dry that Alexander nearly missed it.  
Niall raised the practice knife beside his head. "This is a half-turn throw. At this distance, the blade rotates once from release to target. Your elbow leads. The wrist stays quiet. Power comes from the shoulder and the shift of your weight, not from snapping the hand like a whip."  
He demonstrated the motion slowly without releasing.  
Alexander copied him.  
"No," Niall said.  
"I have not thrown yet."  
"Your wrist is bent. If you release from there, the knife will tumble."  
Alexander straightened it.  
"Better. Now look at the target. Not at the whole board. Choose one mark."  
Alexander selected a dark knot just below the center ring.  
"Do not try to force the point into the wood," Niall continued. "Repeat the same movement and let the distance complete the turn."  
"That sounds suspiciously like letting chance do the work."  
"Chance does enough without being invited. Distance, balance, and repetition do the work."  
Niall stepped back. "Throw."  
Alexander drove the knife forward with the same conviction he used to finish a fencing attack.  
The blade turned twice, struck the target broadside, and fell into the mud.  
The sound was not impressive.  
Niall looked at the knife on the ground, then at Alexander. "That was bad."  
Alexander stared at him.  
Niall's expression did not change.  
"You threw with your wrist, overpowered the release, and leaned so far forward that anyone standing behind you could have pushed you into the target."  
"You might have begun with one fault."  
"There were three."  
"I noticed."  
"Good. Then the lesson is working."  
Alexander waited for the familiar retreat: for the apology, the softened praise, the reminder that first attempts were difficult, and that His Lordship had shown excellent promise.  
It did not come.  
Niall walked to the target, retrieved the knife, wiped the mud from it, and returned.  
"Again," he said.  
Alexander took the blade.  
The second throw struck point-first but glanced away without holding.  
"Your distance changed," Niall said.  
"I did not move the line."  
"Your release moved forward. Half a foot changes the rotation."  
The third throw hit hilt-first.  
"Too early."  
The fourth missed the board entirely and struck the stone wall with a clang.  
Niall closed his eyes briefly.  
"I believe Mr. O'Brien heard that."  
"He is probably taking notes."  
"He will charge the damage against my wages."  
"I will tell him it was my throw."  
"That may double the charge."  
Alexander retrieved the knife himself this time, waiting for Niall's permission before crossing the chalk line. He returned to position and examined the blade as though it had personally betrayed him.  
"You said the knife turns once," he said. "How do you know it will?"  
"Because I know the length, balance, distance, and speed of my own movement."  
"And if one of those changes?"  
"Then the throw changes."  
"In the cavern, the distance was thirty-one yards. The footing was poor. There was smoke. Your movement could not have been the same as in the yard."  
Niall became still. Until then, the lesson had kept his attention upon hands, feet, and safety. The question returned Scotland to the space between them.  
"No," he said. "It was not the same."  
"Then how did you make the throw?"  
Niall looked toward the target rather than Alexander. "Badly."  
"My mother is alive."  
"The result and the method are not always equally fine."  
Alexander waited.  
Niall rubbed his thumb once along the edge of the bandage at his palm.  
"I knew how the knife turned," he said. "I knew the distance well enough. I had trained the movement until my hand could complete part of it without asking permission from the rest of me. The ground shifted. I released late. I struck higher than I intended."  
"But you struck him."  
"Aye."  
"Were you afraid?"  
The word might have offended another man. Niall considered it.  
"More afraid than I had believed possible."  
Alexander watched him. Men spoke of courage often in his presence. Officers described charges after dinner. Hunters told stories in which danger existed mainly to improve the teller. Tutors praised Roman generals for clean decisions made across two thousand years of distance. None had ever admitted fear so simply.  
"My father said you saved her," Alexander said.  
"I did what was needed after waiting longer than I should have. Both things are true," Niall said, realizing how much he sounded like Edward.  
"He did not tell me that part."  
"It is mine to tell."  
The answer was not defiant, but it belonged wholly to Niall.  
Alexander turned the practice knife in his hand. "What were they hunting?"  
Niall's attention sharpened.  
The castle walls seemed suddenly present around them: the barred stable passage, the high windows, the old stones holding every sound.  
"Your father told you what he is willing to have known," Niall said.  
"He told me almost nothing."  
"He told you there were illegal hunters, dangerous restraints, armed resistance, and deaths."  
"And nothing about the thing valuable enough to justify all of it."  
Niall's face gave away less than Alexander expected from someone only three years older.  
"I cannot answer that."  
"Cannot or will not?"  
"Both."  
Alexander felt irritation rising. "You are very direct for a secretary."  
"I am a poor liar. Directness saves time."  
"You could invent an answer."  
"Then you would know I had lied, and I would still have broken your father's confidence."  
"So you prefer to tell me openly that you are keeping something from me."  
"Aye."  
The burr thickened around the word.  
Alexander stared at him.  
It was infuriating.  
It was also the first honest thing anyone had said about the limits of the prior morning's explanation.  
Niall met his gaze. "I will not tell you that you imagined the wagon, or the injuries, or the parts of the account that do not fit. You did not. But the matter belongs to the Duke, and I have been in his service less than a month. I will not repay his trust by deciding what his son may know."  
"He has already decided that."  
"Then your disagreement is with him."  
Most men would never have said it.  
Alexander looked away first, toward the dark knot on the target. "You are not much older than I am," he said.  
"Three years can be a great distance when one man holds a knife and the other is standing in front of him."  
"You know what I mean."  
"I do."  
"Does everyone become this evasive when they enter my father's service?"  
"I was evasive before. It may be why he hired me."  
Alexander laughed despite himself. The sound surprised them both.  
Niall's mouth moved at the corner, not quite a smile. Then he pointed to Alexander's feet. "Your right heel has crossed the line."  
Alexander glanced down. It had. "Again?" he asked.  
"Again."  
The next throw hit the board but failed to stick.  
"Do not chase the point," Niall said. "Repeat the motion."  
Alexander threw again. The knife turned once and struck at an angle, hanging for a heartbeat before dropping.  
"Closer," Niall said.  
"That almost sounded like praise."  
"Do not become dependent upon it."  
They worked through the six blades. Niall corrected the position of Alexander's elbow without touching him, using the flat of another practice knife as a guide. He made Alexander rehearse the release with an empty hand ten times, then twenty. When Alexander attempted to increase the speed, Niall stopped him and returned him to the beginning.  
"You cannot outrun a bad movement," he said. "You only teach the mistake to arrive sooner."  
Alexander's shoulder began to ache.  
The target acquired a pattern of shallow marks around its outer rings, none near the knot he had chosen. Mud gathered upon the knees of his trousers from retrieving fallen blades. Twice he had to resist the temptation to look toward the upper windows where a servant or family member might be watching.  
Niall noticed.  
"Does an audience improve your aim?" he asked.  
"Usually."  
"Then imagine the wall is deeply impressed."  
"The wall has received more of my knives than the target."  
"It has reason to be attentive."  
Alexander took his place once more.  
The first excitement had gone. What remained was irritation, concentration, and the stubborn refusal to let an object of iron make a fool of him.  
He raised the knife.  
"Too much tension in the hand," Niall said.  
Alexander loosened his grip.  
"Choose the mark."  
He found the dark knot.  
"Shift the weight. Elbow first. Quiet wrist."  
Alexander slowly breathed out. Then he threw.  
The knife left his hand without the violent effort of the earlier attempts. It turned once through the clear afternoon light and struck the target below the center ring.  
The point entered the wood.  
The hilt sagged, but the blade held.  
Alexander did not move.  
Niall examined the angle.  
"Your release was still late," Niall said. "And you dropped the elbow."  
Alexander turned slowly toward him.  
Niall allowed the silence to stretch. Then he nodded once. "Better."  
The word carried more weight than a dozen polished compliments.  
Alexander looked back at the blade. It was not centered. It was not straight. It would have been an unremarkable throw from anyone experienced. But it was his.  
Niall crossed the line and pulled it from the target. When he returned, he offered the knife hilt-first.  
Alexander accepted it. "Again?" he asked.  
Niall checked the clock. The hour Edward authorized was nearly gone. Fatigue had begun to show around his eyes, and one hand rested unconsciously against his injured ribs.  
"One more," he said.  
The final throw struck flat and fell.  
Alexander stared at it in disbelief.  
Niall laughed.  
It was the first unguarded sound Alexander had heard from him: brief, warm, and immediately cut short when the movement pulled at his ribs.  
"That," Niall said, "is why one good throw proves nothing."  
Alexander went to retrieve the blade.  
"You enjoyed that."  
"A little."  
"You are meant to encourage the student."  
"I was not aware I had been appointed your tutor."  
"You have spent an hour issuing instructions."  
"One lesson by the Duke's permission. That is not an appointment."  
Alexander returned the practice knife to the table, laying it beside the others in the same direction Niall had arranged them. "Tomorrow?"  
Niall looked at him.  
Beyond the wall, the castle carried on with its ordinary sounds: a cart crossing the inner court, stable doors closing, a distant bell marking the hour. Somewhere beneath those sounds, covered machinery waited behind a locked door, and his parents kept a truth Alexander could feel without yet seeing.  
Niall could have retreated into formality. He could have said that the Duke only authorized one lesson, that his duties belonged to the private office, that the heir had fencing masters already.  
Instead he gathered the practice blades into the wooden box.  
"Tomorrow, then."
