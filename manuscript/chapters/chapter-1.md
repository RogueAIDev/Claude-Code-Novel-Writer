# **Chapter 1**

# The Harvest Festival

## Sterling Castle, Norfolk - Autumn 1851

***

The fiddle was still finding its pitch when Alexander reached the gate, sawing the same three notes up and down as if it meant to argue them into agreement. Beyond it the yard had already filled. Trestles had gone up under the chestnuts in the night, and the air was thick with wood smoke and trodden straw and the heavy, sour must of cider, with something sweeter from the kitchens laid over the top of it all.

From the gate the whole of Sterling Castle could be read at once, the way he had been taught to read any house before he went into it. The great square keep came first, because it was oldest and plainest and had been built by men who did not expect to be visited. Beside it rose the chapel spire, and after the spire the long eastern range ran away along the edge of the plateau, its leaded windows full of morning. The stone was quarried and dressed and weathered to the colour of a hayrick in October, and below the walls the ground fell steeply into forest that no Sterling had thinned in living memory. Past the forest the water-meadows lay under a soft haze, so that the land seemed to go on being Sterling land until it ran out of sight. This morning someone had tied harvest ribbons to the old iron rings along the gate wall, red and yellow, and they lifted and fell in the small wind as though the wall had agreed to be festive for one week of the year and no longer.

The cider cart had gone in to the axle just inside the gate, where the ground was always soft and where, every year, somebody forgot that it was. Mr. Fenwick stood beside it with his hat crushed in one fist, a short square man whose voice had been made for calling cattle across three fields and had never been told it could be used for anything smaller.

"Hold hard, bor. Hold *hard*. That's a full load, and if she goes over I'll have every drop out of somebody's hide."

The estate hands were already at the back of the cart, four of them, red-faced and leaning. Alexander did not stop to consider it. He set his shoulder to the tailboard beside them, found the place where the wood was wettest and the grip worst because the good places were taken, and pushed when they pushed. Over his bent back two of the men exchanged a look. He caught it at the edge of his sight and chose not to.

The wheel came up out of the mud with a sound like a boot leaving a bog. The cart rocked, settled, and rolled on toward the trestles with every barrel still aboard, and Mr. Fenwick put his hat back on in order to take it off again.

"Much obliged, Master Alexander. Much obliged, sir."

"Not at all, Mr. Fenwick."

He was the tallest of them by half a head and the only one whose hands were soft, and there was a splinter in the heel of his hand, a long dark one, and a bloom of cider across his waistcoat. It was the charcoal waistcoat, and his valet would mourn it. He had tied his cravat a good deal looser than fashion asked, as he did most mornings now, and nobody had yet said a word about it, which was the whole value of a rebellion small enough to deny. He worked the splinter out with his thumbnail while the hands went back to their barrels. They liked him. He knew they liked him. They had liked him since he was small enough to be lifted onto a hay wain at harvest, and they would no more have called him by his Christian name than they would have sat down in his father's chair.

Across the yard his father stood at the judging table in his shirtsleeves.

Edward Sterling, seventeenth Duke of Norwich, was a solid, imposing man rather than an elegant one, built by years of work out of doors, and he stood the way he always stood, as though expecting weather. His dark hair was greying, and had gone to silver at the temples. His face was weathered brown, and there were old pale scars at the temple and along one cheekbone that had been there for as long as Alexander could remember and about which nobody had ever told him anything at all. A watch chain crossed his waistcoat. In the other pocket his left hand turned something small over and over, as it did whenever he was thinking, and on the table in front of him lay four marrows of heroic size.

"Weight, Your Grace," Farmer Hodge said. He was a tall, stooped man with a neck like a turkey's and the absolute certainty of the righteous. "Always has been weight."

"It has always been weight, Mr. Hodge, because you have always grown the heaviest. That is a tradition. It is not an argument."

"Girth's naught but show. Any fool can blow a marrow up with water." Hodge did not look at the Widow Carrow's entry, the second from the left, with such force that everyone else did.

His father considered this. He gave it exactly the pause, grave and unhurried, that Alexander had seen him give to letters arriving under seal from London, and when he spoke again it was in the voice of a man setting a matter of Crown revenue before a committee. "Then we have two questions before this table, and not one. Whether the marrow is large, and whether it is honest."

A ripple ran round the people nearest the table, something short of laughter, the sound tenants made when the Duke said something they would repeat at their own fires that night, and Alexander, who had stood at the head of a good many tenants' dinners and been listened to politely at every one of them, recognized it as a thing he had never once been given.

His father looked up then and found him across the yard. The eyes were brown, as Alexander had been told all his life his own were, though he had never seen in any glass the patience that sat in them. Edward gave him a short nod, the nod for a duty seen to, and went back to the marrows.

His mother had placed herself at the end of the preserves table. It was near enough to hear every word of the marrow dispute and a careful yard too far to be appealed to by either side. Amelia Sterling was slender and composed, and wore the festival as she wore everything, as though it had been arranged for her convenience and she was too gracious to say so. Her dark auburn hair was dressed close beneath a straw bonnet, and her eyes, which were green, were fixed on her husband with the fond attention of a woman watching a dog she loved dig up a flower bed. She had taken off one glove to taste Mrs. Davies's damson cheese. When she lifted that hand to shade her eyes, the light found the fine silver-grey lines across her palm, which Alexander had stopped seeing years ago, the way one stops seeing the crack in a familiar ceiling.

"Should someone rescue him?" he asked, coming up beside her.

"My darling, your father has never in his life needed rescuing from a marrow." She put the glove back on, finger by finger. "Let him have his war. It's the only one I allow him."

"Is it Mr. Hodge's war, or the widow's?"

"It is the marrow's war. The rest of them are simply the ground it is fought over." She looked at his waistcoat. "And you have been pushing carts."

"One cart."

"One cart," she agreed, in a tone that suggested she would be hearing about it from his valet.

Thomas Barrow found him a few minutes later at the cider trestles. The under-steward was a big man in a coat a good deal too formal for the work he did in it, fair-haired and freckled to the knuckles from twenty summers out of doors, with a tally book that looked like a prayer book in his hands. He touched his hat.

"Eight barrels in, sir, and two more coming from Lowther's once the cart's been seen to. I've told Mr. Fenwick he's not to put his own on the end of the trestle, but he will." Thomas ran a thick finger down a column. "And you'll be glad to hear the second cut came in off Harrow's Fen yesterday. All of it dry."

"I'm glad of it," Alexander said, because it seemed the thing to say.

"So'll they be." Thomas tipped his head at the yard without looking up from his book. "That's eleven families with hay to see their beasts through till spring, off that one meadow. Folk round here call it useless land, being wet half the year. His Grace won't sell it. Never would." He made a mark in the tally book. "There's my two barrels now."

He went off toward the gate. Alexander stayed where he was.

Harrow's Fen had been a line in his father's ledgers for as long as he had been old enough to read them, a figure for rents and a figure for drainage and a note in the margin about the sluice. He looked out past the wall to where the haze lay over the water-meadow and then back at the yard, and found that he was counting. Mr. Fenwick, still shouting. The Davies boys fighting over whose turn it was on the swing hung from the lowest chestnut bough. The Widow Carrow, small and upright beside her disgraced marrow, who kept one cow and would keep it through the winter because of a field Alexander had never once walked to the end of. He got as far as seven before he lost track of who belonged to which household, and he stood a moment longer in the smoke and noise trying to recover the other four.

The fiddle had found its note. Someone had begun to clap along.

He went to find his sister.

He took the kitchen passage the way he always took it, keeping to the left-hand wall, past the stillroom and the butler's pantry, whose door he did not look at, and came out into heat. Mrs. Thompson's kitchen on a festival morning was a country of its own, with its own laws and a single sovereign. She was a broad woman of fifty or so, floured to the elbow and red to the shoulder, and she conducted four girls and a boy with a wooden spoon the way his father conducted the marrow dispute, with the difference that nobody in her kitchen would have dreamed of answering back. Ribbons hung from the drying rack above the range. The girls went round Alexander as water goes round a post in a stream. Mrs. Thompson herself, passing with a sheet of pastry over one arm, reached up without breaking stride and plucked a straw out of his hair.

"Shows up something terrible on black, that does, love," she said, and was gone.

His sister was sitting on the great table.

She sat in the middle of it, among the cooling racks, in a gown of figured blue silk that had no business within a mile of a kitchen, with a jam tart balanced on her knee and a crescent of flour on one cuff. Catherine was nineteen and had never in her life been still. One heel drummed against the table leg. Her hands were busy breaking the edge off the tart in small pieces and not eating them. Her auburn hair had come down on one side into the waves it was always escaping into, and the heat of the ovens had put colour in her face, so that the little dark mark beside her left eye stood out like a full stop. Her eyes were their mother's, the same green, and quicker.

She saw him, and arranged herself. She straightened her back, folded her hands over the tart, and inclined her head with a precision that would have satisfied the most exacting governess in the county, and that none of her own governesses had ever once managed to get out of her.

"Master Alexander," she said. "You have brought the yard in on your waistcoat."

"Cider. Mr. Fenwick's cart went in at the gate."

"It goes in at the gate every year." Her laugh came before she had finished, as it always did, so that the end of what she said had to climb over it. "I think he does it on purpose, so that someone will come and push and he may shout at them in public."

"Off my table, love," Mrs. Thompson said, passing with a tray. "I've pastry wants that end."

Catherine moved six inches along the table, which seemed to satisfy honour on both sides. "Did you know," she said to Alexander, "that in Saxony they make a crown out of the last sheaf of the harvest, and carry it into the church with ribbons on it, and hang it up there all winter until it's quite dry and the mice have had half of it? I read it. A whole crown made out of—" Her eyes went past him to the window, and stayed there.

He waited. "Out of wheat," he offered.

"Hm? Oh. Yes." She looked back at him as though he had come in that moment, and smiled, and put the tart into his hand. "Here. You've earned it, pushing carts."

He ate it standing up, as he had not eaten anything in a kitchen since he was ten years old. When he glanced at the window to see what she had been looking at, there was only the drying yard and the line of washing and, beyond the wall, the road going away between its hedges toward Norwich.
